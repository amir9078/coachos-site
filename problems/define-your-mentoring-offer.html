Years of mentoring, and still no real answer when someone asks what you offer

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

A woman at my nephew's christening asked what I do and I gave her about forty seconds of something before I heard myself say "it depends". Eleven years I've been doing this. I can tell you exactly what happened with any one of them — the guy who moved out of sales into product, the woman who finally left her firm — but I can't tell you what the thing is that I sell. My rate hasn't changed since 2019 because I wouldn't know what to say to justify changing it.

Someone asks what you do. You say you mentor people, they nod, and then comes the follow-up — what does that actually look like? — and you hear yourself start three sentences and finish none of them. You end up saying something like "it depends on the person", which is true, and which tells them nothing at all.

You are not bad at this work. You could describe, in detail, what happened with every person you have worked with. The one who arrived convinced she wanted to leave the industry and ended up running a team inside it. The one who needed six months of somebody telling him he wasn't mad. You know exactly what you did in each case. What you can't do is say the thing in one sentence, the same way twice.

The gap shows up in specific places. A former mentee wants to recommend you to a friend and stalls, because all they can offer is "he's great, you should talk to him". A prospective client asks what your programme involves and you improvise a version of it live on the call. Someone asks your rate and you name a number that is really a guess about what this particular person will pay.

Why the offer never gets defined

Mentoring practices almost never start as a product. They start because somebody asked for help and you gave it, and then somebody else asked. Each engagement got shaped around the person in front of you, which is the reason the work is good and also the reason it resists description. There is no point anywhere in that path where someone makes you sit down and write a definition.

The second reason is harder to admit. Naming an offer means excluding people. If you say "I work with technical people moving into their first leadership role", you have just told everybody else you are not for them. When the calendar isn't full, that feels like walking away from money. So the description stays wide, and wide descriptions don't stick in anyone's head.

There's a third thing underneath both: you have no idea what to charge for something you can't name, so you avoid the naming in order to avoid the pricing conversation with yourself.

What the vagueness actually costs

The clearest cost is money. The 2023 ICF Global Coaching Study found that more than half of coaches and mentors worldwide earn under $30,000 a year from their practice, even though the average session fee sits around $244 an hour. Those two numbers only fit together one way. Most practitioners are not short of a viable rate — they are short of volume and consistency. A defined offer is what turns a rate into a filled calendar, because it gives people something specific to agree to instead of an open-ended relationship of unclear length.

The second cost is referrals. A referral is a sentence travelling between two people while you are not in the room. If the sentence is "she does coaching, sort of, I think for leadership stuff", it dies on the way. You will never hear about the ones that died, which is why this cost stays invisible for years.

Third, enquiry calls turn into free consulting. With no defined thing on the table, the call has to double as diagnosis, scoping and pitch all at once. You spend an hour giving away the useful part and then have nothing concrete to close on. Do enough of those and you start quietly dreading them.

Why the usual fixes don't hold

The standard advice is to pick a niche. People go away for a weekend, fill a whiteboard, come back with "I help ambitious mid-career professionals find clarity and purpose", and nothing changes — because that sentence describes a feeling, not a situation. Nobody wakes up and searches for clarity. They search for how to tell their boss they don't want the promotion.

The other common attempt is cosmetic: a new about page, a personal brand exercise, a logo. All of it describes a person rather than a transaction. Someone reading it still can't tell what happens if they hire you, how long it lasts, or what it costs.

Both fail in the same place. They're written from the outside in — from what sounds sellable — rather than from the pattern that already exists in the work you've done.

What a working setup looks like

Start backwards. Take the last ten or fifteen people you've mentored and write four things for each: the state they were in when they arrived, what they were trying to do, what actually changed, and how long it took. Don't summarise. Use their words where you remember them.

A shape shows up. Usually two or three clusters, one of which is bigger and more repeatable than the rest. That cluster is the offer, and it's defined by the before-state, not by your method. "Senior engineers who've just been handed a team and are quietly drowning" is an offer. "Leadership coaching" is a category.

Then give it edges: a length, a rhythm, what's included and what isn't, and a price attached to the whole thing rather than to the hour. Write one sentence a former mentee could repeat accurately at dinner without you there to correct them. Put all of it on one page somebody can forward.

You can still work flexibly inside that container — most good mentors do. The definition isn't a cage. It's the thing that lets someone find you and understand what they're agreeing to before the first call, which is the only way the work reaches anybody who didn't already know your name.
