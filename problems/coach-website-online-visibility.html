Every mentee so far has come through someone who already knew the mentor personally

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

Every single person I've mentored came through somebody who already knew me. Old colleague, someone's sister, a bloke from a course I did in 2018. I bought the domain about two years ago and it's still parked — the renewal email lands every March and I think, right, this month. Meanwhile there are people typing "find a mentor" into Google at eleven at night and not one of them will ever get anywhere near me.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

Sit down and list everyone you've mentored. Next to each name write how they found you. Old manager. Someone's sister. A person from a course. A friend of a client from four years back. Every line traces to somebody who already knew you, or knew somebody who did.

That isn't a failure. It's how most practices start, and warm introductions convert better than anything else will. The problem is what the list implies about everyone who isn't on it. Somewhere tonight a person is typing "how do I find a mentor for" into a search bar and finishing the sentence with their exact situation. They've already decided they want this. They're looking with intent, at half past ten, on their own. And there is no possible path from that search to you.

Meanwhile the domain sits parked, the renewal email arrives once a year, and you think about it for about four minutes.

Why the site never gets built

A referral-fed practice never hits the wall that forces the issue. Every mentee so far arrived warm and mostly pre-sold. In your direct experience a website has never once been the deciding factor in an engagement, so it stays a theoretical good rather than an urgent one. Nothing breaks. It just doesn't grow.

The stated reasons are real too. In a Proponent Group survey of coaches, 41% had no business website at all, with 57% pointing to lack of time and 43% to cost. Both are true and neither is the whole story, because building a single page isn't a large job. What makes it large is everything it sits on top of. You'd need the offer defined. You'd need testimonials you've never asked anyone for. You'd need to settle on a price you'd be willing to put in writing where former clients can see it. Each of those is its own unfinished thing, so a page becomes a project with four blockers instead of an afternoon.

There's a quieter reason as well. A thin site feels worse than no site. You'd rather be unfindable than look like you're not busy.

What being unfindable costs

The people you never meet are the ones with the highest intent. A referral is someone who happened to hear about you. A searcher has already reached the conclusion that they need a mentor and is spending their evening trying to find one. You are invisible to precisely the group that's easiest to work with, because they arrive having already made the hard decision.

Second, your ceiling is one network, and networks decay. People change jobs, drift, run out of friends to send your way. A practice growing only through introductions grows at the rate introductions happen, which is a rate you don't control and can't forecast.

Third, you lose the ability to set terms before the conversation starts. Referred people arrive with expectations set by whoever referred them, including about price. Without a page stating what you do and what it costs, every enquiry begins from nothing and every negotiation happens live, while you're improvising.

Fourth, people check anyway. Anyone considering paying you will search your name before the first call. Finding nothing doesn't disqualify you, but it does mean the referrer's one sentence has to carry the entire weight of the decision on its own.

Why the usual attempts stall

"I'll just use LinkedIn" is the common substitute. That profile is rented space, it surfaces for your name rather than for the problem someone is actually searching, and it's structured around your employment history instead of what you help people with. It works for people who already know who you are.

The opposite failure is the big build: twelve pages, a blog, a rebrand, new photography. It dies somewhere around page three and the domain stays parked for another year.

Templates and page builders solve the wrong problem. They remove the technical work, which was never what stopped you. What's left is the writing, and the writing is blocked on the offer not being defined.

What a working setup looks like

One page, live this month, beats a site next year. It needs five things: who you work with described by their situation rather than by a category, what actually happens when someone works with you including length and rhythm, what it costs or at minimum a range, two or three specific accounts of what changed for real people, and one clear way to start.

Own the domain and the email address on it. Rented profiles are fine as signposts. A page you own is where the signposting should end.

After that, add two or three pages built around the phrases people genuinely type. Not "leadership coaching" — the situation, in their words. Someone who just inherited a team searches for what that feels like, not for a service category they've never heard of.

Make it referrable. The point isn't only to be found cold; it's to hand the person recommending you a link that does the explaining they can't do themselves. And keep it current — availability, prices, a recent quote — because a page that's obviously two years stale reads worse than no page at all.
