The mentors everyone wants are usually the ones already closest to burnout

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

At one point I had six mentees, plus the two who aren't officially mine but message me anyway. All of it sits in the 7am to 9am slot because that's the only gap left. Last Wednesday I ran a session where I genuinely could not remember what we'd talked about the time before — I was scrolling my own notes while she was speaking. She said it was helpful. That's the part I keep thinking about.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.
Mentor overload produces what researchers term "marginal mentoring," disengaged and low-value, as demand rises against the same limited pool of willing senior staff. Harvard Business Review, "Don’t Let Mentoring Burn You Out," 2022

You know the four names. Every programme has them. When a mentee describes what they're looking for, two of those four fit, and either the matching surfaces them or a manager recommends them by name. They say yes, because saying yes is how they became those names in the first place.

Check what they're actually carrying. The formal count in the platform is three or four each. The real count includes the ex-colleague they still talk to, the two people from the graduate scheme who message on Teams, and whoever got told "just grab twenty minutes with her" last month. None of that appears in workload planning. Most of it happens before nine or after six, because that's the only space left.

What follows is well documented. Harvard Business Review's 2022 piece "Don't Let Mentoring Burn You Out" describes how mentor overload produces what researchers term marginal mentoring — relationships that are disengaged and low-value — as demand keeps rising against the same limited pool of willing senior staff. The relationships don't stop. They hollow out first.

Why demand concentrates instead of spreading

Mentoring demand doesn't distribute evenly, because mentees don't choose at random. They choose by reputation, and reputation compounds. One good relationship produces a recommendation, which produces two requests, which produce two more recommendations. Matching systems and programme managers both optimise for the same signals — willingness and past responsiveness — so the reward for having mentored well is being asked again.

The people at the centre of this are often the ones for whom declining is most expensive. In a senior population that isn't very diverse, the visible women and people of colour carry a disproportionate share of requests from anyone looking for someone who understands their situation. Saying no feels like closing a door that was closed to them.

And none of the load is counted. Mentoring sits outside the job description, outside workload planning, and usually outside the performance conversation except as a line of praise. A manager reviewing capacity sees the project portfolio, not six hours a month of other people's development. So when something has to give, mentoring is never what gets protected — it's what gets absorbed.

What it costs before anyone actually burns out

The failure mode isn't a dramatic exit. It's degradation. The mentor keeps every appointment and arrives with nothing prepared, scrolling back through their own notes while the mentee is mid-sentence. Advice gets more generic. The session turns into a check-in. Both people feel it, neither names it, and the mentee rates it fine on the survey because the mentor is a good person who showed up.

Then the structural risk. If a meaningful share of your programme's capacity sits with four people, one sabbatical, promotion, parental leave or resignation removes a whole cohort's support at once. There's no gradual handover, because there was never a bench.

There's also a cost to the mentors that programmes rarely track. The people doing the most mentoring are frequently the same people doing the most of everything else invisible — the interview panels, the ERG leadership, the new-joiner onboarding, the awkward conversation nobody else will have. The programme quietly taxes exactly the group the business is most anxious to keep.

Why the standard fixes don't hold

Capping formal pairs at two or three looks responsible and changes very little, because the informal load is the larger half and a cap doesn't touch it. You've limited the part you can see.

Recruiting more mentors broadly is right in principle and slow in practice, because new volunteers don't get picked. Mentees choose known names, so an expanded pool sits idle next to four overloaded people, and after a cycle of never being matched those new volunteers quietly withdraw. You end up with a bigger list and the same four calendars.

Recognition schemes — mentor of the year, a shout-out at the all-hands — actively make it worse. You've just increased the visibility of the person already receiving too many requests.

And advice to "set boundaries" or "learn to say no", however kindly meant, relocates an organisational design problem onto the individual least able to solve it without cost.

What a setup that spreads the load looks like

Count all of it, formal and informal, and put it somewhere a line manager can see. Load that isn't visible can't be balanced, and the first honest audit of who is mentoring whom is usually the most useful hour a programme owner spends.

Put a broker between demand and mentors. When requests go straight to the senior person, the senior person has to do the declining, and declining is the expensive part. When requests route through the programme, saying no becomes an administrative decision rather than an interpersonal one.

Change the default format. A one-to-one is the most expensive possible way to answer a question that thirty people share. Group sessions, cohort circles and structured office hours let a scarce mentor reach many people in the same hour, and keep the one-to-one for cases that genuinely need it.

Time-box relationships with a real ending — a fixed number of sessions, a stated finish, a proper close. Open-ended pairings accumulate forever and capacity never comes back. An ending everyone expected isn't a rejection, and it frees a slot for someone waiting.

Match need to level rather than to seniority. Someone two years ahead is often the better mentor for an early-career question, and they aren't the person everyone else is asking.

Then check on the mentors the way you check on the mentees. Most programmes survey one side. The side you aren't asking is the side that quietly determines whether the programme still exists next year.
