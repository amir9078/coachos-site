A slow site loses people before they see what is on it

Website & PresenceSourced & verified

We ran ads for three weeks and I kept checking the numbers on my phone at night, wondering why the clicks weren't turning into anything. Turned out the homepage took about nine seconds on a phone. On my laptop in the office it was instant, which is exactly why I never caught it — I'd only ever looked at it there. There was a video header nobody asked for and a hero photo that was 8MB. So we paid for a few hundred people to look at a white screen.

The ads are running. The click-through rate looks fine. You paid for the traffic and the ad dashboard confirms the traffic arrived. But the enquiries aren't there, and when you open your own analytics the session count is nowhere near the number of clicks you were billed for.

The click and the visit are two separate events, and there's a gap between them. Someone tapped your ad, got a white screen, waited, and went back. From the ad platform's side, that's a click you owe money for. From your side it never happened. That person doesn't appear in your funnel, doesn't fill in your form, and never forms an opinion about your business, because they never saw it.

Google and SOASTA's "The State of Online Retail Performance" research, published via Think with Google, found that 53% of mobile visits are abandoned once a page takes longer than three seconds to load, and that the probability of a bounce rises 90% as load time goes from one second to five. Three seconds isn't a stretch target. It's the point where half the people who asked to see your page stop asking.

Why sites get slow

Nobody builds a slow site on purpose. Sites get slow the way a garage gets full — one reasonable decision at a time, none of which felt like the problem.

The biggest single cause is usually images. A photo straight off a phone or a camera is several thousand pixels wide and several megabytes heavy. Your site displays it in a box 600 pixels across, but the visitor's phone still downloads the whole original and shrinks it after the fact. Put four of those on a homepage and you've asked someone on a train to download something closer to a video file than a web page.

The second cause is accumulated third-party scripts. A chat widget, an analytics tag, a second analytics tag someone added and forgot, an ad pixel, a cookie banner, a reviews badge, a booking embed. Each one reaches out to a different company's server, and your page often waits on the slowest of them before it will show anything. None was a bad idea individually. Together they mean your homepage can't render until seven other businesses have responded.

The third is how the site was built. Page builders and heavy themes ship the code for every feature they offer, not the ones you use. And all of this gets judged on the wrong hardware: your desktop, on office wifi, with the whole site already cached from the last time you looked at it. Your visitor has a three-year-old phone, a patchy signal, and no cache at all.

What it costs

The clearest cost is paid traffic, because there the waste has a price tag. You're billed per click, not per page view, so every abandoned load is money spent on somebody who saw nothing. Raising your budget doesn't help — it buys more of the same abandonment.

The quieter cost is referral traffic, which is usually your best traffic. Someone recommends you in a group chat and pastes the link. The person who taps it is already warm, already curious, and already predisposed to like you. A blank screen spends that goodwill on nothing, and unlike an ad click you never even knew it existed.

There's also a compounding effect in search. Slow pages are harder for search engines to crawl efficiently and speed is a factor in how pages are assessed, so a slow site is fighting for rankings it would otherwise get. Meanwhile the visitors who do arrive bounce, which is the opposite of the signal you want to be sending. None of it produces a complaint. Nobody emails to say your site was slow. They just don't come back.

Why the usual fixes fail

The most common response is installing a caching plugin. Caching helps with how fast the server responds, but it does nothing about how much the visitor has to download. A cached 8MB homepage is still an 8MB homepage. Aggressive settings also tend to break something visual, so the plugin gets half-disabled, and the site ends up with the complexity of a fix and none of the benefit.

The second is testing it yourself and concluding it's fine. It is fine — for you, on that machine, on that connection, with that cache. Almost every slow site in the world feels fast to the person who owns it.

The third is a one-off cleanup. Someone compresses the images, the site gets quick, and then over the next six months new photos get uploaded straight from a camera roll and a new widget gets added for a campaign, and you're back where you started without noticing the slide.

The fourth is the redesign. A redesign is where speed problems are usually created, not solved, because redesigns are when video headers, animation libraries and full-bleed photography get added. Plenty of sites relaunch slower than the thing they replaced.

What a working setup looks like

Start with a weight limit for the homepage and treat it like a rule rather than an aspiration. Every image gets resized to the size it's actually displayed at before it's uploaded, saved in a modern format, and anything below the first screen loads only when someone scrolls to it. That one habit fixes most sites.

Then audit the third-party scripts and make someone name, out loud, what each one is for and who reads its output. Whatever survives that question stays; the rest come off. Limit fonts to the weights you genuinely use. Check that your host's response time is reasonable, since nothing else can start until the first byte arrives.

Finally, change how you test. Use a real mid-range phone, on mobile data, with the cache cleared, and do it on a schedule rather than once. Speed isn't a project you finish — it's a number that drifts, and the only reason it drifts unnoticed is that nobody is looking at it.
