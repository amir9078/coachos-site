Undercut by $5 gigs, then charged a commission on top

Community & MarketplacesSourced & verified

There's someone in my category offering what looks like the same thing for fifteen quid. Fifteen. I know it isn't the same thing and my repeat clients know it isn't, but the person scrolling at half ten at night has no way of telling. So I went from £120 down to £85 to see what happened — more orders, less money, and then the twenty percent comes off that too. I've put it back up since. Mostly.

You open your own category page and scroll it like a buyer would. There you are, a few rows down, next to someone offering what reads like the identical deliverable for a fifth of what you charge. Same words in the title. A thumbnail that is arguably better than yours. Four hundred reviews.

You know the work isn't equivalent. Your existing clients know it isn't. But the person doing the scrolling has never bought this service before, has three tabs open, and has no way to tell the difference except the number. So they click the cheap one, and either they're happy, in which case you have lost a client you never met, or they're not, in which case they come to you six weeks later annoyed and expecting the cheap price.

Then you make a sale, and a fixed slice of it leaves before you see it.

Why the price collapses

A marketplace listing strips out almost everything that justifies a professional rate. The diagnosis conversation, the judgement about what the client actually needs, the fact that you have done this particular thing for this particular kind of business eleven times — none of that survives the format. What survives is a title, an image, a star rating and a price. Of those, price is the only one a stranger can compare without effort, so price is what gets compared.

The ranking mechanics then close the loop. Listings that convert and sell in volume get shown more. Cheap listings convert and sell in volume. Being shown more produces more sales, which produces more ranking. A new seller with no reviews has every reason to start below what the work costs them, because the first ten orders are not really orders, they are the price of existing on the page at all. And because supply is global while the cost of living is not, there is always someone for whom your floor is a comfortable ceiling.

Sitting on top of that is a commission that doesn't move. FreelanceCompare's breakdown of Fiverr's fees notes the platform takes a flat 20% of every package, every extra and every tip — so a $100 sale nets the seller $80 before tax or payout charges. The rate is the same whether you are the cheapest listing on the page or the most expensive, which means the market pushes your price down and the commission takes its full share of the smaller number.

What it actually costs

The obvious cost is margin. The less obvious one is what you do to compensate for the missing margin, which is take more work. More clients at lower prices is not the same business at a smaller scale — it is a different and worse business. More onboarding, more scheduling, more revision rounds, more people who bought on price and therefore treat the purchase as low-stakes, more of your week spent on admin that doesn't improve with volume.

And that eats the thing that would have let you charge more. Deep work with a small number of clients is what produces the results, the testimonials and the specific expertise that justify a higher rate. Volume at the bottom of the market is what prevents you from ever developing them. People rarely price their way out of this; they work their way further into it.

Why the usual fixes fail

Adding a premium tier feels like the answer, and it does help a little, but buyers still compare listings on the entry price. Your $400 tier is invisible until someone has already decided to open your page rather than the cheap one. Loading up on extras runs into the same commission, since it applies to extras as well as the base package.

Climbing seller levels improves your visibility, which is a distribution win rather than a pricing win — you get more of the same price-led traffic. Niching down inside the platform helps more than most tactics, but it is limited by the fact that the buyer still arrives through a list where everything looks broadly the same and one field is a number. And simply raising your prices only works if demand is reaching you from somewhere other than that list. Otherwise you are just moving down the page.

What a working setup looks like

The pattern that breaks the loop is boring and it is always the same: the buyer needs to arrive already half-convinced, from somewhere that isn't a comparison grid. Someone who found you through a referral, a piece of writing that answered their exact question, or a direct message that showed you understood their situation is not sitting there mentally comparing you to a $15 listing. They are deciding whether to work with you.

Practically that means a few things. Refuse to quote a number before you have had a short scoping conversation, even a written one, because the conversation is where the difference between you and the cheap listing becomes visible. Make your proof specific rather than general — the result for one named type of client beats a list of adjectives. And price against the outcome the client is buying, not against what the person three rows down is charging for a superficially similar task.

Keep the marketplace if it feeds you, but treat it as one channel with known economics rather than the market itself. The listing is a shop window on a street where everything is discounted. What you actually want is people walking in because they were sent.
