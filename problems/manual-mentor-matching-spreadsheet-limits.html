The spreadsheet that worked at 30 people stops working entirely past 60

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

Cohort one was 32 people and I did the matching in an evening with a cup of tea. Cohort two was 58 and it took a whole weekend, two screens, one with the export and one with the tab where I'd colour-coded who got paired last round. Sunday night I found I'd matched someone with her own line manager and had to redo about a third of it. Cohort three is meant to be 90. I haven't worked out what I'm doing about that yet.

Cohort one was thirty-two people and you did the matching in an evening. It was, honestly, quite satisfying — a sign-up export, a few columns for function and seniority and what people said they wanted, some sorting, and out came a list of pairs that mostly made sense. You knew half of them personally. Wherever the sheet was ambiguous, your own judgement filled the gap.

Cohort two was fifty-eight and it took a weekend. Cohort three is scheduled at ninety and you have started quietly dreading it. Not because the work got three times harder — because it got harder in a way that does not track the headcount. Somewhere between the two you stopped being able to hold the whole thing in your head, and once that goes, every decision has to be checked against every other decision.

The specific failures are always small and always obvious afterwards. Someone matched inside their own reporting line. Someone paired with the person who interviewed and rejected them last year. A mentor who agreed to two mentees appearing on four rows. You catch most of these. The ones you do not catch arrive as an apologetic email in week three.

Why matching gets harder faster than the group grows

This breaks for structural reasons, not because of effort. Doubling the participants does not double the work — it squares the space you have to search. Thirty mentees and thirty mentors gives nine hundred possible pairings; sixty and sixty gives thirty-six hundred. Meanwhile the constraints multiply independently of that: department conflicts, reporting lines, seniority gaps, language, location, capacity limits, people who mentored last cycle and asked not to be paired into the same function again, and the handful of sensitive exclusions you were told about verbally and are holding in your head.

Vendors who build in this space describe the same ceiling. MentorcliQ and Together Mentoring Software both put manual spreadsheet matching as realistic only under roughly 25 to 50 participants; beyond that, they describe it as an administrative burden that does not scale, consuming hundreds or thousands of admin hours. That range is not arbitrary. It is roughly where a competent person stops being able to verify their own work by looking at it.

Past that point you are not really matching any more. You are producing an assignment that satisfies whichever constraints you happened to remember while you were looking at that particular row.

What the spreadsheet actually costs

The obvious cost is time — days of a senior L&D person's attention, priced at a senior L&D person's salary, spent on work that is essentially clerical. It recurs every cycle, and it recurs in full, because almost nothing from the previous round carries forward.

The larger cost is that the whole program now sits on one person's undocumented knowledge. The file has colour codes only they understand, a tab labelled "old" that is actually the current one, and formulas nobody else has read. When that person is on leave, or leaves, the program does not degrade gracefully. It stops. The next cohort slips a quarter while someone reverse-engineers a workbook.

And there is a quality cost that never appears as a cost. Under time pressure you stop optimising and start satisficing — the first acceptable pairing rather than the best available one. Participants experience that as a mediocre match, disengage, and the program's outcomes drop for reasons that look, from the outside, like participant apathy.

Why the obvious fixes stall

Building a better spreadsheet is the first move and it fails predictably. Weighted scoring formulas, conditional formatting, a macro someone's colleague wrote. This raises the ceiling a little while making the file more fragile and more personal, which worsens the key-person problem you were trying to solve.

Self-serve matching — publish the mentor list, let mentees choose — moves the labour rather than removing it. The visible, senior, well-liked mentors are oversubscribed within a day and everyone else gets nothing, so you rebalance manually anyway, now with the added awkwardness of telling people their first choice is full.

Buying dedicated software solves the algorithm and leaves the operation. Matching is maybe a third of the administrative load. The rest is chasing incomplete sign-up forms, following up mentors who never confirmed, rescheduling, answering the same eight questions, and finding out which pairs have actually met. A platform will store that information. It will not do it for you.

What a setup that survives cohort three looks like

  • Write the constraints down before you match. Hard rules that can never be broken, soft preferences that can. A constraint that exists only in your memory does not exist for anyone covering your work.
  • Collect structured input, not free text. "What do you want from mentoring?" as an open box guarantees a manual read of every response. Fixed options plus one free field gets you most of the nuance for a fraction of the handling.
  • Separate matching from onboarding. Most of the pain attributed to matching is actually chasing — incomplete forms, unconfirmed mentors, silent participants. Run that as a continuous process before match day and match day becomes short.
  • Keep a human on the exceptions. Automated matching handles the bulk cleanly and gets a small number of cases wrong in ways only a person recognises. Review the outliers, accept the rest, stop reviewing everything.
  • Make the process legible to a second person. Not documentation for its own sake — just enough that a colleague could run a cycle if you were unavailable for three weeks.

The goal is not a perfect algorithm. It is that cohort four does not require your weekend.
