No real website, so a prospect can't verify you're legitimate

Website & PresenceSourced & verified

Someone asked for my website on a Tuesday and I sent them my Instagram. She said thanks, that's great, and then just — nothing. I'd been telling myself the site could wait because everything came through referrals anyway, and it did, mostly. It's the ones that don't come through that you never hear about. The domain's been sitting in a cart since March.

A friend passes your name along. Before that person messages you, they search you. Not carefully — they type your name and maybe the word "coach" or your city into their phone, look at whatever comes back, and decide in about the time it takes to read a menu.

If what comes back is an Instagram grid, a LinkedIn profile untouched since your last job change, and a Facebook page you created by accident in 2019, they don't consciously think "this isn't a real business." They think something vaguer. They think hm, and they go back to their messages, and the referral dies without anyone finding out. No bounced email. Nobody tells you. The person who referred you assumes it went fine, and so do you.

Clutch's "State of Small Business Websites" report found that 17% of small businesses still had no website of their own as of 2025, down from 36% in 2018. The direction matters more than the number. When most businesses had nothing, having nothing said nothing about you. Now that most have something, having nothing says something — and what it says isn't accurate.

Why the website never gets made

It's almost never because you don't understand why a website matters. It's because a website is the one piece of business infrastructure with no natural deadline. A client call has a time on it. An invoice has a due date. A tax return has a penalty attached. A website has nothing forcing it, so every week it competes against work that pays this month, and every week it loses. Three years go by and no individual decision was ever wrong at the moment you made it.

The job also grows every time you look at it. It starts as "put up a page." Then you remember you need photos, and you don't like the photos you have. Then you need to write about yourself, which is genuinely hard and which most people are worse at than they expect. Then someone mentions booking systems and testimonials and a blog for search traffic. By the time you've finished thinking about it, the job is a month of work, so you put it down again.

The quietest reason is that social platforms feel like they're already doing the job. You post, people reply, some of them book. That feedback is immediate and it's real. What you can't see from inside that loop is everyone who searched for you outside the platform, found nothing they trusted, and never entered the loop at all. A platform reports on the people it reaches. It tells you nothing about the people it doesn't.

What it actually costs

The first cost is the silent verification failure. It's invisible by design: the prospect who fails to verify you doesn't complain, doesn't reply, and doesn't appear in any dashboard. You experience it as referrals being slower than expected, or as a vague sense that people take a long time to make up their minds about you.

The second cost is structural, and it's the one that ends businesses rather than slowing them down. If your entire presence lives in one account on one platform, you're renting your identity from a company whose support process is an automated form. Accounts get suspended for reasons nobody explains. Accounts get hacked. Accounts get caught in enforcement sweeps aimed at somebody else. When that happens to a business whose website is its Instagram, the client list, the message history, the enquiry inbox and the proof of existence all go at the same moment.

Then there are the smaller costs. Without a domain you have no email at your own name, so contracts and invoices go out from a Gmail address. Without a site of your own, the pages that rank for your name are pages other people control — an old directory entry, a marketplace profile with a five-year-old bio, a review page. You're findable, but the story is being told by whoever bothered to write it down.

Why the usual fixes don't hold

The link-in-bio page is the most common substitute and the weakest. It's another rented account. It doesn't rank in search, it answers none of the questions a hesitant buyer has, and it looks like what it is — something set up in four minutes.

The custom build fails the opposite way. It gets scoped properly, a designer is engaged, and then it stalls at the content stage, because the content requires you to write clearly about what you do and what you charge. That's a task you can defer indefinitely without anyone noticing. Half-built sites have sat behind "coming soon" pages for years for exactly this reason.

The bought template fails a third way. It goes live with the demo copy still in it — a stock photo of a boardroom, a heading that says "Your Story Here," a team page listing three invented staff. That's worse than nothing, because it doesn't read as new. It reads as abandoned.

What a working setup looks like

Small and finished beats large and pending. You need a domain you own, registered in an account with your name and your card on it rather than your web designer's. Email at that domain. And a site that answers five things plainly: who you work with, what actually happens when someone hires you, how pricing works, what evidence exists that this has worked before, and how to book. One page can carry all five if it's written honestly.

Two details do most of the credibility work. The first is a sign of life — a dated post, a current availability note, anything proving the site was touched this year rather than in 2021. The second is consistency: the same name, photo and description on the site and on every profile pointing to it, so the person checking you out lands in the same place twice and stops checking.
