Generic positioning gets lost in an increasingly crowded field

Marketing & VisibilitySourced & verified

Someone asked me at a networking thing what I do and I said "executive coaching, mainly leadership and confidence", and watched her face do absolutely nothing. Which is fair — the other two coaches in the room said more or less the same. My website says it as well, just with better photos. I keep meaning to sit down and work out what's actually different about what I do, and then a client emails.</p>

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

Read your own about page as a stranger would. Somewhere in the first two lines there is a phrase like helping leaders reach their potential, or supporting people through transition, or partnering with ambitious professionals. It is true. You do those things. It is also almost word-for-word what several hundred other people have written, and the reader has no way to tell you apart from any of them.

This is not a copywriting problem, and rewriting the sentence with better verbs will not fix it. The sentence is vague because the underlying decision — who this is specifically for and what specifically changes — has not been made, and the writing is doing its honest best to describe something undefined.

The uncomfortable part is that vague positioning used to be survivable. When there were fewer people offering the same thing, being generally good and locally known was enough to get found.

Why everyone's positioning converges on the same words

The International Coaching Federation's 2025 Global Coaching Study, drawing on more than 10,000 respondents across 127 countries, put the number of coach practitioners worldwide at a record 122,974, with the global coaching market at $5.34 billion. Both figures are records. That is the room you are describing yourself in.

Convergence happens for reasons that each make sense individually. Training programmes teach a shared vocabulary, so graduates describe their work in the language they were taught — which is, by design, the same language everyone else was taught. Broad claims feel safer commercially: naming a specific situation feels like turning away everyone who does not match it, and when the pipeline is thin, narrowing is the last thing anyone wants to do. And most practitioners genuinely can help a wide range of people, so writing something narrower feels dishonest rather than strategic.

There is also a simple visibility trap. Generic positioning is invisible to you, because you know what you mean when you read it. You supply all the context the stranger does not have. The sentence reads as full of meaning on your own screen and as noise on theirs.

What undifferentiated positioning actually costs

The first cost is that consistency stops rescuing you. Posting three times a week, showing up in the right groups, doing the newsletter — all of it produces less than it should, because each impression fails to be memorable. Frequency multiplies whatever your positioning is. Multiplying zero gets you nothing, which is why so many people conclude their content is not working when the content is fine and the description of the work is the problem.

The second cost lands on referrals, which is where most practice work comes from. A past client wants to recommend you but cannot say who you are for, so they only mention you when someone describes a need in exactly the words the client remembers. Precise positioning turns your network into people who can spot your prospects on your behalf. Vague positioning limits referrals to moments when someone says out loud that they want a coach.

The third is price. Interchangeable descriptions invite comparison on the only remaining variable, which is cost. When a buyer cannot tell what is different, they negotiate — reasonably, since from their seat nothing else distinguishes the options.

Why the usual fixes do not land

Picking a niche is the standard prescription, and it usually gets applied as a demographic label: coaching for female founders, coaching for tech leaders. This narrows your audience without saying anything about the work, so you end up with the same generic promise addressed to fewer people. Worse commercially, no clearer to the reader.

Tagline exercises have a similar failure mode. A better-crafted sentence about the same undefined offer is still an undefined offer, now more confidently phrased.

Credential-stacking — more letters, more accreditations, more logos — answers a question buyers ask late, not the one they ask first. Qualifications reassure someone who has already decided you might be right. They do not create that decision.

And more output makes the underlying problem more expensive rather than less, since you are paying the production cost repeatedly for impressions that do not stick.

What specific positioning actually contains

Useful positioning is not a cleverer sentence. It is four decisions, made explicitly, that the sentence then reports.

  • A situation, not a demographic. Not "senior leaders" but the recognisable circumstance — first ninety days after a promotion into managing former peers, or a founder handing over delivery to a first hire. People search and describe themselves by situation.
  • A named before and after. What was true when they arrived and what is true when they leave, in their words rather than developmental language. Concrete beats aspirational.
  • Something you deliberately exclude. If nobody reads your positioning and thinks "that's not me", it is not positioning. Exclusion is what makes the inclusion legible.
  • Evidence you actually have. The situations you have handled repeatedly, the pattern you can describe better than anyone else in the room. This is usually where the differentiation already exists — most practitioners have it in their client work and have never written it down.

The test is whether a past client could repeat it accurately a week later. If they can, your positioning is doing work while you are asleep. If they can only say you are very good, you are back to competing on being memorable, in a field of a hundred and twenty thousand people who are also very good.
