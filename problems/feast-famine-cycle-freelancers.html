The feast-or-famine cycle repeats because marketing only restarts once the pipeline is already empty

Marketing & VisibilitySourced & verified

January and February I was turning work away. By April I was down to two clients and a lot of free afternoons, so I started posting and emailing people again. That work landed in June — by which point I was flat out and stopped doing any of it. Fourth year running. Same shape every time, roughly the same months.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

The pattern has a shape you could draw. Busy stretch, everything running, no time for anything that is not client work. Then a quieter month that feels like a break. Then a month that does not feel like a break, because two contracts ended and nothing replaced them.

So you start marketing. Properly, this time — you post, you email people you have not spoken to in a year, you follow up on old proposals. It takes four or five weeks and then it works. Enquiries come in, two convert, and you are busy again.

And because you are busy again, the posting stops, the emails stop, the follow-ups stop. The work you did in the quiet month is still producing results for a while, which conceals what is happening. Then it stops producing, six weeks after you stopped doing it, and you are back at the start.

The cycle is built into the timing, not the discipline

MBO Partners' 2023 State of Independence in America report found that "not enough predictable income" was the single biggest challenge independents named that year, cited by 49% and up from 43% the year before. Half the independent workforce, and the number moving in the wrong direction. Whatever is going on here, it is not a personal failing distributed across millions of people. It is structural.

The structure is a mismatch between two clocks. Delivery is immediate: the session is today, the deliverable is due Friday, the money follows within weeks. Marketing is delayed: what you do this week produces enquiries in four to eight weeks, sometimes longer, because a person needs to see you several times before their own circumstances line up with getting in touch.

Now put those clocks in one calendar with one person and no spare capacity. Marketing effort is highest when demand is lowest, because that is the only time there is room for it. The results of that effort arrive later, when you are busy. Being busy removes the capacity for marketing. So the input goes to zero at precisely the moment its output peaks, and the drought that follows is already locked in six weeks before you feel it.

That is why it repeats. It is not that you forget. It is that the correct time to market — during the feast — is the time you have least to spare, and the wrong time to market feels most urgent.

What it costs beyond the empty months

The income gaps are the visible cost and not the largest one. The larger costs come from what a thin pipeline does to your decisions.

Pricing goes first. When you have one live conversation and a gap in the calendar, the discount request is much harder to refuse, and you often pre-emptively quote lower than you would have in February. The same work gets sold at different prices depending on when in the cycle the conversation happens.

Client selection goes next. In a famine you take on the poorly-fitting client, the one you would have politely declined in a good month. That client takes disproportionate time and energy, which crowds out marketing, which extends the cycle — and often produces a mediocre outcome that generates no referral.

Then there is the compounding you never get. Businesses with steady acquisition build an audience over years, so each round starts from a warmer base. A stop-start pattern resets: audiences cool, contacts go stale, momentum drains between pushes. Four years of intermittent effort does not accumulate into four years of reputation. It accumulates into four separate cold starts.

Why discipline-based fixes keep failing

The most common advice is to time-block marketing during busy periods and protect it. This fails for the same reason the original problem exists: the block has no counterparty. When a client needs to move a session into that slot, the marketing block moves, every time, because one of those two things has a person waiting and the other does not.

Retainers are usually offered as the answer, and they do help with revenue smoothing. They do not fix acquisition. A book of retainers still ends — contracts finish, budgets get cut, someone changes role — and if acquisition stopped while the retainers were running, the same drought appears with a delay.

"Market when you are busy" is directionally right and useless as instruction, because it prescribes an outcome rather than a mechanism. The person who could reliably do a demanding second job during their busiest weeks was never going to have this problem.

The other reflex — panic marketing when the calendar empties — has a particular flavour that hurts conversion. Outreach written from need reads differently to outreach written from interest, and prospects can feel it.

What actually interrupts it

The only durable fix is a level of marketing activity that does not depend on your available capacity. Not more marketing — marketing that survives your worst week.

  • Set the floor at your busiest month, not your quietest. Whatever you can genuinely sustain in a full week is the real number. A plan built for a light week is a plan that switches off exactly when it matters.
  • Take production off your calendar. Your judgement is needed for what to say. Drafting, scheduling, list maintenance, and follow-up sequencing do not require you, and while they sit on your desk they compete with delivery.
  • Track the lag deliberately. Note when output dips. A gap in activity in March explains a gap in enquiries in May, and seeing that written down breaks the habit of blaming the market.
  • Handle follow-up systematically. Most of the pipeline in a famine already exists — old proposals, past clients, dormant enquiries. That work is repetitive and does not need originality, which makes it the easiest part to keep running.

Steady beats intensive here, not because it produces more in total, but because it removes the six-week hole you dig each time you go quiet.
