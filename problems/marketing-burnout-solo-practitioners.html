Doing marketing solo quietly becomes a second full-time job

Marketing & VisibilitySourced & verified

I do client work during the day and the marketing after nine at night, so the marketing gets whatever's left of me. That was fine for about eight months. Then I missed the Friday newsletter twice, and after that I stopped writing captions properly and just posted the photo. I wouldn't say I burned out, exactly. I just went quieter and quieter until there wasn't much left going out.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.
77% 77% of people doing social media as their actual job report active or recent burnout, and 45% are considering leaving the field. Link in Bio, 2025 Social Media Salary and Career Survey (2,500+ respondents, 40 countries)

It does not announce itself. There is no week where you decide the marketing is too much. What happens is that the newsletter you used to write on Thursday afternoon starts getting written on Thursday night, then Sunday, then not at all. The posts get shorter. The caption becomes one line and a photo. You notice, briefly, and tell yourself it is a busy patch.

Underneath it is a straightforward arithmetic problem. You are doing the delivery work — the sessions, the programmes, the client emails — and then you are doing a second job on top of it, in the hours after the first one finishes, with no handover and nobody to cover you.

The second job is real work. It has a name, it has professionals, and it wears them out too.

You are doing a full job as an add-on

Link in Bio's 2025 Social Media Salary and Career Survey, covering more than 2,500 people across 40 countries who do social media as their actual job, found that 77% reported active or recent burnout and 45% were considering leaving the field entirely. These are people with the role written into their contract, working set hours, often with a team and a manager, doing nothing else. Three quarters of them are burnt out.

Now consider what a solo practitioner is doing. The same task — daily output, public performance, constant novelty, immediate feedback from an audience — squeezed into the gaps of a delivery calendar, with no separation between work hours and marketing hours, no colleague to hand a bad week to, and no end to the shift. The surprising thing is not that it becomes unsustainable. It is that anyone expects a compressed, unsupported version of a job that burns out professionals to hold up long-term.

Three features make it heavier than it looks. It never finishes — there is no state in which the marketing is done. It is public, so every post carries a small judgement risk that a spreadsheet does not. And it demands original thought at the exact time of day when you have least of it left, because the client work took the good hours.

The order things fail in

The collapse is predictable and it has a sequence. First quality goes: the thinking gets thinner, the posts become observations rather than anything that took effort. Frequency holds for a while, so from the outside nothing looks wrong. Then frequency drops — three a week becomes one, the monthly newsletter becomes quarterly. Then it stops.

The cruel part of the timing is that the drop-off often lands right when the earlier work was starting to pay. Marketing compounds slowly; the people who have been reading for three months are the ones about to enquire. Going dark at month eight throws away most of what months one through seven built, and starting again later does not resume from where you stopped. It resumes closer to the beginning.

There is a second cost that shows up in the work itself. Someone running on empty at ten at night makes worse decisions about pricing, about which clients to take, about whether to chase a lead. The marketing exhaustion does not stay in the marketing.

Why the usual advice does not help

"Do less, but do it consistently" sounds right and mostly fails, because the effort is not in the volume. Writing one good post still requires deciding what to say, having something to say, and finding a version of yourself willing to say it publicly. Cutting from five posts to two removes some of the production but almost none of the decision-making, which is the tiring part.

Batching helps for a month. The batching session is the same job, done in one sitting, and it depends on the same depleted attention — so it gets postponed once and abandoned twice.

Automation tools tend to add work before they remove it. A tool that generates generic drafts creates a new task: reading generic drafts and rewriting them into something that sounds like you. For many people that is slower than writing from scratch, and it adds a small, constant irritation.

Hiring help works only if it changes what lands on your desk. If the freelancer needs briefing calls, idea lists, and tone corrections, you have not offloaded the job. You have added a management task to it, and management tasks are the first thing to slip when you are already stretched.

What a version that survives looks like

The useful question is not how to be more disciplined. It is which parts of this actually require you, and which parts only require someone.

  • Requires you: the point of view, the client stories, the judgement on what is true and what you would never say. This is a small share of the total time.
  • Does not require you: turning that raw material into drafts, formatting, scheduling, keeping track of what has already been published, checking the rhythm has not quietly stopped.

A setup that holds moves the second category off your calendar entirely and shrinks your involvement to review — minutes, not evenings, and a decision rather than a blank page. It also needs a floor low enough to survive your worst month rather than a target set during your best one, and something outside your own memory noticing when output drops, since the person burning out is the last to spot the pattern.

Marketing that only runs when you have spare energy is not a system. It is a hobby with revenue attached, and it stops the moment the energy does.
