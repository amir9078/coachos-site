Mentoring demand is real and rising. The pool of willing mentors is not keeping pace

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

We opened mentee sign-ups and got 140 in about a week, a lot of them writing really specific things about what they wanted help with. Then we opened mentor sign-ups and got 31, and eleven of those were the same people who did it last year. One replied saying happy to help but she's already carrying three, which I took as a no and I don't blame her. So there's a waitlist I don't advertise, and a group of grads who signed up in April and have heard nothing.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

The demand side was never the problem. You opened sign-ups and well over a hundred people asked for a mentor, most of them in their first few years, most writing something specific about what they wanted help with. It reads well. You could put half of those responses straight into a slide.

Then you opened mentor sign-ups. You got a fraction of that, and a meaningful share were the same people who volunteered last time — the ones who always say yes, who are already mentoring two people informally, who sit on the graduate panel and the internal speaker series and one committee you have forgotten about. One replied that she was happy to help but currently carrying three, which you read correctly as a no.

So now you are rationing. You match the mentees you can, hold the rest in a list you do not advertise, and hope nobody asks in a town hall why they signed up in April and have heard nothing since. Meanwhile the mentors you did secure are being stretched, which is a reliable way to lose them for next cycle as well.

Why the pool is smaller than the org chart suggests

The mismatch is well documented from the demand side. The Adobe Future Workforce Study found that 83% of Gen Z workers say a workplace mentor is important to their career while only 52% report having one, and the University of Phoenix Career Optimism Index found that 56% of American workers overall say they lack a mentor entirely. Appetite is not in question.

Supply is where the mechanism sits, and it has little to do with generosity. Consider what you are actually asking a senior person for. The ask is open-ended — "be a mentor" has no stated end date, no defined time commitment, and no definition of success. It is unpaid in every sense that matters: it does not appear in objectives, it carries no weight in a performance review, and it competes directly against work that does. It carries a quiet risk of doing it badly, which experienced people are far more sensitive to than juniors assume. And it is offered to precisely the population that is already the most over-asked, because the qualities that make someone a good mentor are the same qualities that get them onto every other voluntary thing in the building.

Given all that, the surprising thing is not that the pool is thin. It is that anyone volunteers at all.

What unmet demand costs

The cost concentrates in the population you can least afford to lose. Early-career and Gen Z hires are the group most likely to ask for a mentor, most likely to read its absence as a signal about their prospects, and most likely to act on that signal quickly. They are cheap to replace individually and expensive to replace in aggregate, because the loss surfaces two or three years later as a hollow layer in the middle of the organisation.

The second cost lands on the mentors who did say yes. Over-allocating your willing volunteers is how you convert them into former volunteers. The person carrying three mentees on top of a full role rarely complains — they simply decline the following year, and the pool has shrunk further while you were trying to serve it.

Then there is the credibility cost. A waitlist that never moves teaches people that internal development offers are performative. That belief is expensive to reverse, and it travels through a team faster than any announcement you send.

Why recruitment drives do not fix it

The standard response is to ask harder: an email from a senior leader, a campaign, a line about mentoring being everyone's responsibility. This produces a small bump from people who were already close to yes, and nothing from the rest, because none of the actual barriers were touched. The ask is still unbounded, still unrecognised, still competing with delivery work that has a deadline.

Mandating mentoring for a grade or level is worse. It fills the roster and hollows out the quality, and a mentee can tell inside one session whether the person opposite them chose to be there. A reluctant mentor is not neutral — it is worse than no match at all, because the mentee concludes the problem is them.

Recognition schemes — a certificate, a mention at the annual awards — do not survive contact with a busy quarter. They address status, and the binding constraint is time.

What actually widens the pool

  • Shrink the unit of commitment. "Six conversations over six months, forty-five minutes each, then it ends" converts a lot of maybes into yeses. Open-ended asks read as unlimited liability.
  • Stop assuming one-to-one. Group mentoring, cohort sessions and topic-based clinics let one senior person serve several mentees in the time a single pairing would take. Not identical value, but far better than a waitlist.
  • Widen who counts as a mentor. Peer mentoring one level up handles a large share of what early-career people actually want, which is practical, recent and tactical. Reserve your scarcest seniors for the questions only they can answer.
  • Take the admin off the mentor's side. Scheduling, reminders, prep, notes. Every minute of overhead you push onto a mentor is a minute taken out of the conversation and a reason not to return next cycle.
  • Buy capacity where internal supply genuinely cannot stretch. External coaches for specific populations is not an admission of failure. It is what you do when demand is real and internal supply is finite.
  • Make the contribution visible where it counts. If mentoring never appears in a promotion case, senior people learn exactly what it is worth.

The pool is not fixed. It is priced — in time, risk and recognition — and most programs have set the price too high without ever noticing they set one.
