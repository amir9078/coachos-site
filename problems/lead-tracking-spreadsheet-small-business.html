Somebody enquires on a Tuesday. It comes through the Instagram account, or a form, or a number a friend passed on, and you answer from your phone between sessions because the reply is short and you know the answer. You mean to write it down properly that evening.

Three months later you come across the thread while looking for something else. She replied once. You never saw it, because that conversation was never in a place you look at deliberately — it was in an app you open for other reasons.

Meanwhile there's a spreadsheet. Two hundred-odd rows, a status column that was accurate for the first forty of them, and a date column that stops around the point the business got busy. The uncomfortable part isn't the rows you can see. It's that you have no way of knowing how many enquiries never made it into the sheet at all.

Why the spreadsheet stops being true

Capture is demanded at the worst possible moment. Leads arrive through direct messages, email, a form, a phone call, WhatsApp, and a conversation at the end of a workshop. There's no single inbox, so nothing collects them on its own. Recording one means stopping — mid-session, driving between appointments, ten minutes before a call — and typing into a tab that isn't open. So it gets deferred, and deferred entry is skipped entry.

The obvious answer, buying software, misses what the software assumes. CRMs were designed for sales teams: pipeline stages, record ownership, reporting to a manager, and somebody whose job includes keeping the data clean. A solo coach or a five-person firm has none of those roles. The licence fee is real and the benefit depends on a job nobody is doing.

So the default holds. Capterra's research on small business CRM adoption found that over two-thirds — 69% — of small and midsize businesses use no CRM software at all, with roughly a third still tracking leads in spreadsheets. A spreadsheet is a fine list. It's a poor system, because it only ever contains the leads you had spare capacity to type into it, which is the wrong sample of your leads.

What losing the record actually costs

At the individual level it's blunt: contact details and context, gone, with no record it existed. You never find out whether that was a small piece of work or the biggest client of your year.

The larger cost is that the loss is unmeasurable, which turns every decision downstream into a guess. You can't tell whether the ads are working, whether referrals convert better than the form, or whether the enquiries you dropped were the good ones. Budget then gets set by feel.

Then there's follow-up, which is where a lot of the money sits. Enquiries rarely buy on first contact. Without a record of who is owed what, you follow up with whoever you happen to remember — usually the most recent or the most enthusiastic, which is not the same thing as the most likely to buy.

And there's nothing to hand over. When you finally get help, the pipeline lives in your head and your phone, so the first weeks of any assistant's time go on reconstructing it.

Why the usual fixes fail

  • Buying a proper CRM. The software was never the missing piece; entry was. An unmaintained CRM is a more expensive empty spreadsheet, and it usually gets abandoned within a couple of months.
  • A better spreadsheet. Colour coding, dropdowns, a tidy template — all of which raise the effort of adding a row, which reduces the number of rows added.
  • Using the inbox as the system. Search is excellent at finding a thread you remember and useless at surfacing the one you forgot, which is the exact failure you're trying to fix.
  • A one-off automation. Form to sheet, a notification, an autoresponder. It works until a field is renamed or a new channel appears, and because it fails silently you find out months later.
  • A VA with no defined intake. They inherit the ambiguity rather than resolving it.

What a working setup looks like

The requirements are unglamorous and short.

  • One destination, no exceptions. Every channel ends up in the same record. If a lead can live in two places, it will end up in neither.
  • Capture at the moment of contact — automatic where the channel allows it, a few seconds of effort where it doesn't.
  • Four fields. Who they are, where they came from, what they asked for, and the next action with a date. Everything else is optional and mostly ignored anyway.
  • Every lead has a next action or is explicitly closed. Closed is a legitimate state, and marking things closed is what keeps the list trustworthy enough to open.
  • A ten-minute weekly review of anything overdue. Short enough to survive a bad week.
  • Monitoring, not just storage. Something that flags a lead sitting with no next action, because silent failure is precisely how the last setup broke.

None of this needs expensive software. It needs capture to stop depending on someone remembering, at the end of a long day, to open a tab.
