A booked call is not the same as a call that happens

Sales & ClosingSourced & verified

The two o'clock on Thursday is the one that gets me. I clear the hour before it to read the intake form, make a coffee, sit there with the link open. Then nothing, and at twelve past I send the "no worries, want to find another time?" message I've got saved in my notes app because I send it so often. It's about one in four. And it's never the flaky-sounding ones — it's usually whoever seemed keenest on the booking form.

Two o'clock on Thursday. You blocked the hour before it to read their intake form and look up the company. The link is open, your notes run down the side of the screen, and the question you want to open with is written at the top so you don't lose it. Five past: nothing. Twelve past: you send the message you keep saved somewhere, because you send it often enough to have saved it. No problem at all — shall we find another time?

Usually there's no reply. Once in a while someone answers three weeks later, and by then whatever made them book has gone cold.

The irritating part isn't really the hour, although the hour is real. It's that a booked call reads as progress. It sits in the calendar, it gets counted as pipeline, it makes the week look solid — and then some portion of it evaporates on the day, without warning, at five past two.

Why a booked call and a call that happens are different events

Booking is almost free. It takes eleven seconds on a scheduling page, at the exact moment someone is reading your site and feeling motivated about the problem. Attending costs half an hour, requires them to have prepared nothing but still look competent, and lands in a week that looks nothing like the week they pictured when they clicked. The same person makes both decisions in completely different states.

Intent also decays, and it decays with the gap. A call booked for tomorrow morning still carries the feeling that produced it. A call booked eleven days out has to survive their sprint, a sick child, and a manager reprioritising the very thing that sent them looking for you. Nothing went wrong. The reason simply stopped being urgent.

Then there's the missing social cost. Standing up a scheduling link does not feel like standing up a person. There is no reception desk, nobody waiting in a lobby, no one they'll run into next month. On a bad morning, the cheapest available action is to do nothing and hope you don't chase.

None of this means your process is broken. Weave and Aexus appointment-scheduling benchmark data treat a 70-80% show rate as a good result for B2B sales appointments, which means 20-30% of booked calls no-show as a matter of course. Even a well-run pipeline loses roughly a fifth to a quarter of what it books. What separates one operation from another isn't whether it happens — it's whether the people who miss ever come back.

What each no-show actually costs

Preparation is the visible cost: the reading, the research, the hour you couldn't give to a paying client because it was blocked out. For anyone who prepares properly — which is most coaches and consultants, because preparation is what makes the call worth taking — that exceeds the length of the call itself.

The larger cost is momentum. Their problem was live when they booked. By the time you get another slot in the diary, if you ever do, they've half-solved it, dropped it down the list, or spoken to someone else. Whatever heat the original enquiry had is gone, and the second conversation starts colder than the first one would have been.

There's a planning cost too. If you count booked calls as pipeline, you're forecasting on a number that overstates what will actually happen, and the correction shows up at the end of the month when you can least afford it.

And then the quiet one. No-shows make you worse at the calls that do go ahead. Two in a week and you start preparing less thoroughly, because why spend an hour on something that might evaporate — and the under-preparation lands on the people who did turn up.

Why reminders alone don't fix it

Most people bolt on an automatic reminder email and consider the matter handled. It helps a little, then stops helping, for a few specific reasons.

  • Reminders inform, they don't re-sell. "Your meeting is in 24 hours" tells someone about an obligation. It says nothing about why they wanted it, and the wanting is the part that decayed.
  • One channel isn't enough. The reminder lands in the same inbox that already has four hundred unread messages — the inbox that swallowed the booking confirmation in the first place.
  • Rescheduling is harder than ghosting. If moving the call means writing an apologetic email and proposing three new times, plenty of people will just not show up instead. Awkwardness beats admin, every time.
  • Nobody chases the miss. This is the real gap. One "sorry we missed you" message goes out at 2:15, gets no reply, and that person quietly leaves your pipeline. They weren't a bad lead. They had a bad Thursday.

What a working setup looks like

Shorten the gap first. The single most effective change most practitioners can make is to stop offering slots eleven days out. Booking windows inside a week hold up much better, because far less has to survive in between.

Ask for something small at the point of confirmation. A one-line reply — what they most want out of the call, or a link to their site — turns a passive booking into a small commitment. People who have typed something to you behave differently from people who clicked once and closed the tab.

Make the last reminder answerable, and put it close to the call. A short message a few hours before, on whichever channel they actually read, that asks something rather than announcing a time. If they can't make it, you want to know at eleven, not find out at five past two.

Make rescheduling one tap, and offer it out loud. You would far rather have the honest move than the silent skip, so remove every bit of friction and embarrassment from the honest option.

Most importantly, treat a no-show as a stage in the pipeline rather than an exit from it. The person who missed on Thursday still has the problem that made them book. They need the same persistence you'd give any warm lead — several attempts, spaced out, each carrying something worth opening — instead of the one apologetic email that everybody sends and nobody answers.
