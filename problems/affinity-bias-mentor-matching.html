Willing, qualified mentors get passed over because matching just means who is already in the room

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

Third year I've put my name on the mentor list. Two matches in that time, both of them people I already ate lunch with anyway. Then the pairings came out in June and a colleague of mine had four — lovely bloke, genuinely good at it, and also the same background as most of the people doing the matching. I'm not saying anybody did anything on purpose. I'm saying nobody had to think about it and it still came out that way.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

You put your name forward again. Third time. The form asked for your job title, your years of experience and a two-line bio. You filled it in on a Tuesday morning and heard nothing for eleven weeks.

Then the pairings come out and you notice something. The people getting matched, repeatedly, are the ones who are physically and socially near the people doing the matching. Same floor, same lunch table, same background. Not exclusively. Enough that you can see it.

Nobody in this process is behaving badly, which is what makes it almost impossible to raise. There's no single decision you could point at and call unfair. There's a series of small, reasonable, four-second judgements, and an outcome that looks like a pattern.

Why matching defaults to whoever is already visible

Informal matching runs on recall. Somebody asks "who'd be good for this person?" and an answer surfaces within a few seconds, pulled straight from working memory. Working memory is stocked by who you've spoken to recently, who sits near you, whose name came up in a meeting last week. It isn't a search of everyone qualified. It's a search of everyone available to mind.

Coqual's 2019 study "The Sponsor Dividend" found that 71% of workplace sponsors and mentors choose proteges who share their own gender or race. That figure is the aggregate result of thousands of individually innocent recall events. It describes what happens when matching is left to instinct at scale, not an accusation about anybody's intent.

Comfort compounds it. Mentoring needs candour early, and people quietly predict that candour will come more easily with someone whose experience they assume resembles their own. The prediction is often wrong and almost never examined, because the pairing was made in the time it takes to answer a question in a corridor.

Where profiles exist but are thin, the same thing happens through a different door. Given only a name, a title and two lines, a matcher falls back on proxies — seniority, familiarity, a company they recognise. Thin data pushes people straight back onto instinct.

What it costs

If you're a mentor outside whichever group dominates the matching, the cost is blunt: you're willing, you're qualified, and you're unused. Worse, you're frequently the person a specific mentee most needs — someone who has actually been through the thing they're facing — and neither of you will ever learn the other existed.

For mentees, the visible cost is a pairing that looks fine on paper and produces nothing. Two people who like each other well enough, meeting monthly, discussing not very much, because the match was made on availability rather than on what the mentee is actually trying to do.

For programmes, the cost hides. The roster looks healthy. Sign-ups look healthy. Activity is low, drop-off is high, and the explanation offered is usually that people are busy.

Then there's the slow cost to the mentor. Being passed over repeatedly reads, internally, as not being good enough. Most people don't complain about it. They just stop putting their name forward, which removes them from the pool and makes the next round look even more like the last one.

Why the usual fixes fail

A statement of intent changes nothing, because it doesn't touch the moment where the decision gets made. The matcher still asks themselves the same question and still gets the same fast answer.

An open call for mentors that collects names and titles reproduces the problem with more paperwork attached. So does rotating the matching committee — it swaps one set of memories for another set of memories.

Quotas applied over an unchanged process create pairings both people can tell were assigned, which is its own kind of failure. The mentee suspects they were a box being ticked. The mentor suspects the same thing, and neither says so.

What a working setup looks like

Replace recall with a list, and make the list carry enough to choose from. A useful mentor profile describes situations rather than status: what you've been through, what kinds of problems you're good with, what you're not the right person for, how much time you have, and how you prefer to work. That last set of details does more matching work than any credential.

Match on the mentee's stated situation and goal. That means collecting the mentee's side in the same structured form and comparing need to experience, rather than person to person.

Let mentees choose from a shortlist instead of receiving an assignment. Self-selection from a structured pool routes around the matcher's memory entirely, which is the part of the machine that was producing the pattern.

Then measure the thing nobody measures: who is never picked. A list of mentors sitting at zero matches after two cycles is the most useful diagnostic a programme has, and producing it takes a query rather than a review.

If you're an individual mentor with no programme to fix, the same logic still applies to you alone. Write the profile yourself and publish it somewhere findable without going through anyone's memory. Describe the situations, not the titles. The point is to exist outside the room where the four-second decisions get made.
