Marketplace fees quietly take a much bigger bite than the headline rate suggests

Community & MarketplacesSourced & verified

I only worked it out because I was doing my tax return on a Sunday and the numbers didn't match what I thought I'd earned. Gross was fine. Then there's the commission, then the Connects I'd bought in that panic way at 11pm because a good job had just posted, then the wire fee every month because I never got round to changing the payout method. It came to more than my rent. I'm not even angry about it, I just genuinely didn't know.

The headline rate is the part everyone knows. Ten percent, give or take, depending on the platform and the year. It gets repeated in every "is it worth it" thread as if it were the whole arrangement, and at ten percent the arrangement sounds fair enough — cheaper than an agency, cheaper than hiring someone to sell for you, roughly the price of not having to find work yourself.

The trouble starts when you add it up properly. Not the commission line on one invoice, but a full year of them: the commission, plus the credits you bought to send proposals, plus the credits you bought at a premium because something good posted late at night and you'd run out, plus the payout fee every time money moved to your bank, plus the currency spread that never appears as a line item anywhere. Most freelancers have never done this sum. The platform does not do it for you, and there is no single screen that shows it.

When you do it, the number is not ten percent.

Why the real rate hides so well

Marketplace fees are unbundled, and unbundling is what makes them hard to feel. Commission is deducted at the moment of payment, so it never registers as an expense you paid — it registers as money that simply never arrived. Bidding credits are bought in advance, in small amounts, in a completely separate mental account; by the time a credit turns into a proposal nobody read, the cost is already sunk and forgotten. Withdrawal fees land at the bank, weeks later, on a statement you skim.

Each of these sits in a different place in your head, and none of them is the number on the pricing page. That is not necessarily a conspiracy. It is what happens when a business charges for four things and markets one of them.

Upwork fee analyses published by Jobbers.io and GigRadar in 2026 put it plainly: a freelancer grossing $60,000 a year pays roughly $7,000 to $12,000 once platform fees, bidding costs and withdrawal fees are counted together — an effective rate of 12 to 20 percent of gross, rising to 22 to 34 percent once you include bidding spend that produced nothing at all.

That second range is the one worth sitting with. The gap between "the fee" and "what this costs me" is mostly proposals that went nowhere, and proposals that go nowhere are not an anomaly. They are the normal operating condition of a bidding market.

What it actually costs

The abstract percentage becomes concrete when you convert it into things you would otherwise have bought. At the top of that range, the fee drag on a $60,000 year is more than a month of income. It is a decent laptop, a year of every piece of software you use, and a holiday, with change. It comes out before tax, before your accountant, before the unpaid hours you spend writing proposals, and before the client who pays late.

There is a second cost that never appears on a statement. When your only source of work charges per attempt, you start behaving like someone who cannot afford to be picky. You bid on things you half want. You quote lower than you meant to because the fee is coming off the top. You take the awkward client because you have already paid to be in the room with them. The pricing pressure and the fee structure reinforce each other, and the outcome is a business that runs hot and nets thin.

Why the usual fixes don't hold

The standard advice is to raise your rates. On a marketplace where the buyer arrives through a list sorted by price and rating, that only works up to a point, and the point is set by the market rather than by your confidence. The second piece of advice is to move long-term clients onto a lower fee tier, which genuinely helps, but only for the small number of clients who last long enough to get there. Most don't.

Taking clients off-platform is the fix everyone whispers about, and it carries real risk: it breaks the terms you agreed to, and losing an account is losing your entire distribution overnight. Bidding harder is the fix people actually choose, and it is the one that makes the arithmetic worse, because the wasted bidding spend is the largest single component of the gap between the headline rate and the real one.

What a working setup looks like

The honest answer is not "quit the platform". For a lot of people the platform is still the cheapest client acquisition available, and it deserves to be judged as a paid channel rather than as a home. What changes things is having a second channel where the cost per attempt is zero.

That means somewhere the client can find you that you own: a site that explains what you do specifically enough for someone to self-select, a list of people who have worked with you or nearly did, a habit of following up with old clients on a schedule rather than when you remember. It also means measuring channels the way an advertiser would — not "how much did I earn on Upwork" but "what did one client from this channel cost me, all in, including the proposals that failed". Once you can see that number per channel, the decision about where to spend your next hour stops being a matter of mood.

The reason most freelancers never build the second channel is not ignorance. It is that the direct channel needs feeding every week whether or not you have time, and the marketplace does not. It is there at 11pm when you need work. That convenience is exactly what you are paying 22 to 34 percent for.
