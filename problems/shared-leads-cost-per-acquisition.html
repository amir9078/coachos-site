Paying for a lead does not mean being the only one who paid for it

Community & MarketplacesSourced & verified

The notification came in at 7:40 on a Tuesday. Paid for it, wrote a proper reply inside about four minutes — not a template, actual sentences about her situation. She already had three others. One of them was half my price and I never heard back from her at all, and the fee doesn't come back either. I still buy them, which is the part I can't really defend.

The notification arrives and there is a small rush to it, because someone out there has described exactly the thing you do and asked for help with it. You pay the fee. You write back properly — not a template, a real reply that references what they actually said. You do this within minutes because you have learned that minutes matter.

Then nothing. Or a polite line saying they have gone with someone else. Or, most often, silence, and three weeks later you notice the enquiry has closed and you have no idea what happened.

What you sometimes find out, if the person mentions it, is that four other businesses got the same enquiry at the same moment and all of them paid for it too. The fee was charged when the lead was delivered. Nobody promised it would be yours.

Why the same enquiry gets sold five times

This is not sharp practice hiding in the small print — it is the business model, stated openly, and it is worth understanding rather than resenting. A lead marketplace makes money as a function of leads delivered multiplied by businesses charged. Selling one enquiry to several providers multiplies revenue per enquiry without any additional cost of acquisition, and from the consumer's side it looks like better service: more quotes, faster, more choice.

The consequence is that the marketplace's incentives and yours diverge at precisely the point that matters. They are optimising for enquiry volume and consumer choice. You are optimising for won jobs. Nothing in their revenue depends on whether you win, which is why lead quality varies so much and why refund policies tend to be narrow. The person who filled in the form at 11pm to see roughly what something costs generates the same fee as the person ready to book.

The arithmetic gets uncomfortable when you follow it through. An analysis of Thumbtack lead costs published by Auto-Respond in 2026 found lead-marketplace fees commonly run $15 to $80 per lead, with each lead distributed to three to five competing businesses who are all charged; at typical conversion rates of 8 to 12 percent, the true cost per acquired customer lands somewhere around $500 to $1,188.

What that means for a small practice

If you sell a package worth a few hundred pounds, the numbers do not work and no amount of hustle fixes them. If you sell something worth several thousand with a decent chance of repeat work, they might work very well. The problem is that almost nobody calculates which situation they are in, because the fees are small and frequent and the wins are large and occasional, and human beings are bad at that particular comparison.

The other cost is the shape it puts on your day. Speed is the main lever you have on a shared lead, so you end up living near the notification. You reply from the car park. You break off a session. You check at nine on a Sunday evening. That is a real tax on attention, and it is paid whether or not the lead converts.

Why the usual fixes fail

Responding faster is the standard advice and it does work, up to the point where everyone else is also responding fast, at which point it is simply the entry requirement and the advantage is gone. Buying more leads to smooth out the variance multiplies whatever your true cost per acquisition is; if the underlying economics are bad, volume makes them worse faster.

Filtering hard so you only buy well-qualified leads is sensible and worth doing, but it reduces volume without necessarily improving the conversion rate much, because the thing that loses you the job is often not fit — it is that someone else replied first or quoted lower. Which leads to the last fix, dropping your price to win the lead you have already paid for. That converts a marketing cost into a margin problem and quietly trains you to sell at a rate you cannot sustain.

What a working setup looks like

Start by measuring the thing that actually matters: fully loaded cost per acquired client, per channel, including every fee spent on leads that went nowhere. Most people track spend and revenue separately and never divide one by the other. Once you have that number, the decision to keep buying leads becomes a business decision rather than a habit.

Then treat the shared lead for what it is — a race decided by how quickly a specific, human first reply lands, not by price. Specific means it refers to what they wrote. A first message that mentions their situation and proposes one concrete next step beats a faster generic one, and it is the only lever that is genuinely yours.

Capture everything into your own records, including the leads you lose. A lead you paid for and did not win is still a person with a real problem in your area, and the person who chose the cheapest quote in March is often the person who needs someone competent in September. Almost nobody follows up on lost enquiries, which is exactly why it works.

Finally, make purchased leads a top-up rather than the whole diet. Every completed job should generate a request for a referral and a reason for the client to mention you. Referred work costs nothing per attempt, arrives pre-trusted, and does not have three other quotes attached to it.
