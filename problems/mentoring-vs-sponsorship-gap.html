Mentoring built for equity rarely translates into who actually gets sponsored

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

We ran two cohorts, a bit over 200 pairs, and the feedback was genuinely warm — not polite-warm, actually warm. Then the promotion list came out in January and I put it next to the mentee spreadsheet and there were two names on both. Two. And the mentors weren't slacking, that's the thing — none of them were in the room when those decisions got made, so what exactly were they meant to do. I still haven't worked out how to say that to the exec team without it sounding like an excuse.

The programme looks healthy on every measure you're allowed to report. Two cohorts, a couple of hundred pairs, attendance holding up, feedback that's genuinely warm rather than merely polite. You have quotes you'd be happy to read out.

Then the promotion list publishes in January and you put it next to the mentee spreadsheet. Two names appear on both. The stretch assignments that came up in Q4 went to people who were already visible. In calibration, the names that got defended were defended by someone who had worked with them directly — a skip-level, a project lead, someone with a stake in the outcome. Almost none of them were defended by a mentor.

The mentors weren't slacking. Most of them weren't in the room where those decisions were made, and the few who were had nothing to offer beyond an impression formed across four one-hour conversations. That's the gap, and it's widespread: only 40% of employees say they have a workplace mentor, and just 23% report having a sponsor — someone who actively advocates for them — according to Gallup's Center on Black Voices research from March 2022, based on a survey of more than 8,000 workers. Advice is roughly twice as available as advocacy.

Mentoring and sponsorship are different transactions

A mentor spends an hour. A sponsor spends credibility. That difference explains most of why one scales and the other doesn't.

Mentoring happens in private, on request, and carries almost no risk to the senior person. If the advice turns out to be bad, nobody else finds out. Sponsorship happens in public, usually unprompted, in rooms the beneficiary isn't in, and it costs the sponsor something real. Push someone forward and have it go badly, and it's your judgement that gets marked down. People are appropriately careful about spending that.

Which means sponsorship has an entry requirement mentoring doesn't. To advocate for someone, you need specifics you'd defend under challenge. "She's impressive" doesn't survive a calibration meeting. "She took over the migration when the lead left in August, and here's what shipped" does. Mentors, by design, usually don't have that kind of evidence.

Why formal programmes rarely convert into advocacy

Most programmes deliberately pair across reporting lines and often across functions. The logic is sound — you want candour, and you don't want the mentee's performance rating tangled up in the conversation. The side effect is that you've systematically matched people with someone who has no vote in their promotion, no line of sight to their work, and no standing to comment on it.

Confidentiality framing compounds it. Programme guidance usually tells mentors that what's said stays in the room. Right for the mentoring relationship, directly opposed to what sponsorship requires — talking about this person elsewhere, favourably, with detail.

Then there's timing. Sponsorship is calendar-bound. Advocacy that lands in March, when calibration happened in February, is a pleasant conversation with no consequence. Mentoring programmes run on a cohort calendar that almost never maps onto the talent calendar, so the conversations that could matter happen at moments when nothing is being decided.

Measurement seals it. Programmes report pairs matched, sessions held, satisfaction scores. Not one of those numbers would look any different in a programme producing zero sponsorship, which is how the gap survives for years inside something that appears to be working.

What the gap costs

The obvious cost is that the pipeline goals the programme was funded against don't move. Representation at senior levels stays flat, the DEI section of the board pack repeats last year's language, and the programme becomes the thing you point at to demonstrate effort rather than result. That question tends to come back at board level with a bit more edge each time.

The less obvious cost sits with individuals. Someone did everything the programme asked. Turned up, set goals, took the advice, built the skills. And watched the promotion round go past them anyway. That's a worse outcome than never having joined, because it burns the explanation — they now believe, with evidence, that effort isn't the constraint. Some of them leave. The ones who leave are disproportionately the people you named in the business case.

What a setup that produces sponsorship looks like

Recruit sponsors separately from mentors, and state the ask plainly. Not "share your experience" but "twice a year, in a room this person isn't in, you'll say their name and cite something specific they did." Some senior people will decline that. Useful information to have upfront rather than discover in February.

Pair for proximity when sponsorship is the goal. The sponsor needs a genuine view of the work, even indirectly — a shared programme, an adjacent team, a project they can pull the person into. Cross-functional distance is right for coaching and wrong for advocacy, and you can run both structures at once instead of asking one relationship to do two incompatible jobs.

Give sponsors something to say. Advocacy in the room fails much more often from having nothing concrete to hand than from unwillingness. A short running record of what the person actually delivered, written in language that works in a calibration conversation, is the most useful artefact in the entire programme — and it's exactly the sort of thing that never gets maintained, because it's nobody's job.

Run it against the talent calendar. Sponsorship conversations should be scheduled backwards from calibration, succession review and staffing decisions, not forwards from a cohort launch date.

And measure what you actually want: stretch assignments given, nominations made, names raised, promotions landed. Keep the mentoring programme too. It does a different and genuinely valuable job. Just stop expecting it to produce an outcome it was never structured to produce.
