Deals that closed needed far more follow-up than most people ever attempt

There is a folder somewhere — an email label, a spreadsheet tab, a stack of cards in a drawer — full of people who were interested. Not politely interested. They asked what the programme costs. They said the timing was awkward this quarter but to come back to them. One of them forwarded your proposal to their finance lead and told you they'd done it.

Then the thread went quiet, and at some point you decided it was over.

Nearly everyone who sells their own work has this folder. The people in it are warm. Most of them still have the problem you were going to be hired to fix. A few have already bought something like what you sell, from someone who kept writing. The gap between those two outcomes is usually not skill, positioning or price. It is the number of times someone was willing to make contact after the conversation stopped being enjoyable.

Why the follow-up stops around attempt two

Silence is ambiguous, and people resolve ambiguity in whichever direction hurts least. A prospect who hasn't replied in nine days might be on holiday, waiting on their own budget approval, or quietly hoping you'll chase because they haven't had a chance to make the internal case yet. They might also have decided against you. Only one of those readings lets you stop, and stopping is the comfortable option, so that is the reading most people take.

The second reason is structural. Follow-up is the one part of selling with no natural trigger. A booked call appears in your calendar. An invoice has a due date. A client session has a client sitting in front of you. Follow-up on a stalled conversation exists only in your memory, and it competes for attention with delivery work that has real deadlines. Delivery wins, and it probably should. That is how a warm lead sits untouched for five weeks and then starts to feel too old to touch at all.

The third reason is that most people badly misjudge how much contact is normal. RAIN Group's Top Performance in Sales Prospecting research, based on 488 buyers representing $4.2 billion in purchases and 489 B2B sellers, found it takes an average of eight touches to get an initial meeting or response from a prospect — and even top-performing sellers need five. If your internal sense of "reasonable" runs out at three, you are quitting in the middle of the normal range and reading it as rejection.

What abandoning a warm lead actually costs

The obvious cost is the deal. The less obvious one is that it was the best deal in your pipeline. The people you give up on early are, by definition, people who already engaged: they know who you are, they have had the conversation, they have seen a number. Replacing one means starting from a stranger and paying the full price again — the introduction, the qualifying, the discovery call, the proposal. You discard the most expensive thing you own, then spend money manufacturing a worse copy.

There is a second cost, and it shows up in how you feel about selling. When you abandon warm leads, your conversion rate from first conversation to signed client looks terrible, because the denominator includes everyone you quietly wrote off. That number then feeds a story: the offer isn't landing, the pricing is wrong, I'm bad at this. So you rebuild the sales page and rewrite the pitch, when the thing that broke was the seventh email that never went out.

And there is the buyer's version of events: you turned up, seemed keen, then disappeared. Whatever they concluded about how much you wanted the work, it was not flattering.

Why the usual fixes don't hold

The first attempt is usually discipline — a note in a diary, Friday afternoons blocked for follow-up. It survives about three weeks, until a client crisis eats a Friday and never comes back.

The second is a CRM with reminder tasks. Better, but it converts one problem into another: forty overdue tasks on a screen, each asking you to remember what the conversation was about and write something worth reading. Reminders tell you that you owe someone a message. They do nothing about the effort of writing it, and the effort is what stopped you in the first place.

The third is a generic automated sequence. It does reduce the effort, and it replaces useful contact with obvious template mail. Warm buyers can tell. Someone who spent forty minutes on a call with you and then receives "Just circling back!" learns that the forty minutes did not register anywhere. Volume without relevance burns a list faster than silence does.

The fourth is handing the chasing to someone junior. That can work, but it tends to fail on judgement. They don't know which prospect needs a nudge this week and which one needs leaving alone until their budget cycle turns over in April.

What a follow-up setup that survives a busy week looks like

The version that holds up has three properties.

  • The next contact is decided while the conversation is still warm. At the end of every call, the plan for that person exists — what gets sent, roughly when, and what each message is for — instead of a vague intention to follow up at some point.
  • Drafting is not your job. The message arrives already written, using what was actually said: the thing about their board, the timing they mentioned, the number they kept coming back to. You edit and approve. Approving takes ninety seconds. Writing from a blank screen takes fifteen minutes, which is precisely why it doesn't happen.
  • Every touch carries something. Eight contacts is only reasonable when they are not eight requests for a status update. A relevant example, an answer to the objection they half-raised, a short note when something shifts in their sector — that can run to eight without wearing out its welcome.

There is one more property, and it is the least comfortable: someone has to keep the pipeline honest. Deals get marked lost because they went quiet, not because anyone said no. A working setup makes you write down the real reason, and a surprising number of those entries read: I stopped.
