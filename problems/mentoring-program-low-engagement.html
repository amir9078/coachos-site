The program is universal. Whether it actually helps anyone is a separate, unasked question

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

We launched in March and 240 people signed up, which everyone treated as the result. Then in October I went through the check-in logs and about half the pairs had met once. Once, and then nothing. The ones going well were going well because two people happened to get on, not because of anything I did. I still put the sign-up number in the quarterly deck, because that's the number people ask for.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.
98% 98% of Fortune 500 companies run mentoring programs, but only 37% of professionals report actually benefiting from them. Harvard Business Review, "Why Mentoring Programs Fail," Dec 2024

The launch went well, which turns out to be the problem. There was a slide at the all-hands, a sign-up form, a landing page comms put together in an afternoon. A few hundred people put their names down. Matches went out in one batch. Nobody complained, nobody escalated anything, and from two levels above you the program is running.

Around month four you go looking for evidence. Not because anyone asked — because you would like to know. What you find sorts into three groups. A small set of pairs meeting regularly and getting something real out of it. A larger set who met once, agreed it was useful, and never rebooked. And a set who never replied to the introduction email at all. None of this is recorded anywhere in the system. You know it because you asked people.

The awkward part is that nothing is visibly failing. Enrolment was strong. The feedback survey came back positive, because the people who fill in mentoring surveys are overwhelmingly the people whose pairing worked. There is no incident, no complaint, no red cell on a dashboard. The budget line renews on schedule. The gap sits there quietly until it surfaces somewhere else entirely — an exit interview, a promotion slate that looks thinner than it should, a function where the two-year hires keep leaving.

Universal adoption is not the same as universal benefit

This gap is not a local embarrassment. Harvard Business Review, in its December 2024 piece "Why Mentoring Programs Fail," reports that 98% of Fortune 500 companies run mentoring programs while only 37% of professionals say they have actually benefited from one. Those two figures describe the same programs from opposite ends: near-total institutional adoption, and a minority of participants who got anything out of it.

The mechanism is not laziness on anyone's part. It is that a mentoring program is designed up to the moment of launch and no further. Everything that produces enrolment — executive sponsorship, comms, a sign-up flow, a matching pass, a kickoff — is front-loaded, visible, and owned by a named person with a date attached. Everything that produces benefit is the opposite. The second meeting, the fourth, the awkward session where the mentee turns up with no agenda and the mentor has nothing prepared: that work is spread thin across twelve months, invisible from outside, and owned by nobody in particular.

So the program does exactly what it was built to do. It creates pairs. Whether those pairs produce anything is left to chemistry — and chemistry works often enough that the successes are real, just not attributable to any design decision anyone made.

What the quiet version costs

The direct cost is the easy part: a platform licence or an admin's time, plus the hours of every senior person who agreed to mentor and then had one conversation. That is money, and money can be recovered.

The expensive part is the false signal. Leadership believes the development question is handled. When retention softens in a particular population, or a promotion round exposes that nobody in a layer is ready, mentoring is not examined — because mentoring exists. A budget line that is assumed to be working absorbs the pressure that would otherwise force a real diagnosis. You lose a year, sometimes two, before anyone asks whether the thing actually reached the people it was meant for.

There is a second cost, subtler. Every employee who signed up hopefully, waited, and heard nothing has now been told something about how seriously their development is taken. They will not raise it. They will simply not sign up next time, and they will mention it if someone asks them on the way out.

Why the usual fixes do not move it

The first instinct is to communicate harder. More reminders, a mid-cycle email, a message from a senior sponsor. This lifts activity for about a fortnight and then decays, because the constraint was never awareness. Everyone knew the program existed.

The second instinct is to mandate. Required check-ins, compulsory session logs. This converts a voluntary relationship into a compliance task, and people comply — they log the meeting they did not have, or hold a fifteen-minute call so the box turns green. The data gets worse, not better, because now it is actively wrong rather than merely absent.

The third instinct is to survey. But surveys arrive after the fact, go to everyone, and are answered by the engaged. A drifting pair does not tell you it has drifted; it simply stops appearing. Absence is the most important signal you have, and it is the one thing a survey cannot capture.

What a working setup looks like

The shift is from running a launch to running an operation. Concretely, that means a handful of things:

  • Instrument the middle, not the ends. The number that matters is not sign-ups and it is not year-end satisfaction. It is how many pairs held a session in the last six weeks. Everything else is downstream of that.
  • Treat silence as an event. A pair that has not met in six weeks should trigger an action — a light, specific prompt to both sides, not a broadcast reminder. Most drifting pairs are not unhappy. They are each waiting for the other person to schedule.
  • Give people something to talk about. A large share of second meetings never happen because neither party knows what the second meeting is for. A short prompt tied to where the pair is in the cycle removes most of that friction.
  • Make re-matching normal. Some pairings do not work. If the only exit is quiet abandonment, that is the exit people will take. An explicit, blameless way to swap keeps participants inside the program instead of outside it.
  • Report the honest number. Active pairs, not enrolled participants. It will be lower and it will be defensible, and it gives leadership something they can actually respond to.

None of this is clever. It is just ongoing, and ongoing is the part that gets dropped, because it is the part that never has a deadline attached to it.
