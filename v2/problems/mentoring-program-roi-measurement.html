A 4.2-out-of-5 satisfaction score is not a number a CFO can act on

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

The finance review is in November and the only real number I have is 4.2 out of 5 from a survey most people ignored. Last year that was fine. This year the CFO asked what would happen if we didn't run it, and I said something about engagement, and I could hear how thin it sounded while I was saying it. The people who got the most out of it are the ones I can't put in a deck — it's stuff like, she didn't leave. I've got no way of showing that was us.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

The finance review is in three weeks and you already know roughly how it will go, because you have last year's deck open. Slide one: participants. Slide two: sessions delivered. Slide three: a satisfaction score, 4.2 out of five, from a survey a minority of the enrolled group bothered to answer. Slide four: two quotes, both good, both from people you personally chased for a quote.

Last year that was enough. This year the CFO asks a different question, which is what would happen if you did not run it. And you say something about engagement and retention, and you can hear the shape of the answer as it leaves your mouth — plausible, unfalsifiable, exactly the sort of thing a person says when they do not have the number.

What makes it worse is that you believe in the program. You have watched it work. You know of at least three people who stayed because of a conversation that happened inside it. You cannot put any of them in a deck, because the evidence is a story, and a story is not a number, and the thing being decided is a number.

Why satisfaction is the number you end up with

Nobody chooses satisfaction scores because they are meaningful. They are chosen because they are the only thing cheap enough to collect and fast enough to arrive. You can send a survey and have a figure by Friday. Everything genuinely worth measuring is slow, diffuse, and contaminated by other causes.

Teleskope, in "How to Calculate ROI on Mentoring Programs," puts it plainly: most HR teams do not have a clear methodology to capture and quantify mentoring impact, and proving it to a CFO or executive team signing off on next year's budget is where most HR and L&D teams struggle. That is not a competence gap. It is a design gap. Measurement was never built into the program, so what remains afterwards is whatever can be reconstructed from memory and a survey tool.

There is also an attribution problem that is real rather than an excuse. If a mentee is promoted, mentoring contributed alongside their manager, their project, a reorg and their own effort. Any honest person knows they cannot claim the whole thing. That honesty is precisely why L&D under-claims and why finance under-values.

What faith-based renewal costs

A program renewed on faith is also cut on faith. That is the entire cost, and it is enough. When budgets tighten, nobody runs an analysis of which programs work. Cuts happen in order of demonstrated value, and a satisfaction score sits near the bottom of that order — below anything with a headcount attached, below anything with a contract, below anything with a figure a controller already recognises.

Meanwhile you cannot improve what you cannot see. Without outcome data you have no basis for deciding whether to extend the program to another population, close the format that is not working, or shift budget between cohorts. You are running on impressions, and impressions are systematically biased toward the loudest participants.

And there is a career cost nobody says out loud. The person who cannot defend their program in finance's language gets less budget next year, regardless of whether the program was good.

Why "measure everything" fails

The common reaction is to measure more: longer surveys, a pulse check, a dashboard with fourteen tiles. This adds burden to participants, lowers response rates, and produces more of exactly the same kind of evidence. Volume does not convert self-report into outcome.

The second reaction is to reach for a large monetary figure — a productivity uplift multiplied across headcount, or a retention saving derived from a replacement-cost assumption. A CFO will find the assumption inside thirty seconds, and once they do, everything else in the deck is discounted with it. An indefensible large number is worth less than a defensible small one.

The third reaction is to measure retrospectively: look back at last year's participants and see how they did. The problem is the counterfactual. High performers volunteer for mentoring. They would have done well anyway. Without a comparison group you are measuring who signs up, not what the program did.

What a defensible setup looks like

  • Choose the outcome before launch, with finance in the room. One or two measures, agreed in advance — retention in a named population, internal mobility, time-to-productivity for a hire cohort. Agreeing the target afterwards always looks like fitting evidence to a conclusion.
  • Build a comparison group. Eligible people who did not participate, or a delayed cohort starting six months later. It does not need to be a clean trial. It needs to be better than nothing, which is what you currently have.
  • Instrument participation as the middle variable. Sessions actually held, per pair. If you can show that pairs who met six times behave differently from pairs who met once, you have a dose-response argument — the most persuasive thing available without a controlled study.
  • Report in units the business already uses. Not "engagement improved." Regretted attrition in the target population, or vacancies filled internally. A number that already appears in someone else's report carries weight yours does not.
  • State the cost side honestly, including mentor hours. Volunteering the full cost buys credibility on the benefit side. Hiding it invites someone else to go looking.
  • Keep the stories, but put them after the number. Anecdote confirms a finding. It cannot substitute for one.

None of this needs a research team. It mostly needs deciding what you intend to prove before the program starts, rather than three weeks before the review.
