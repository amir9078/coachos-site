A one-day coaching workshop rarely survives contact with an actual quarter

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

We did the coaching day in February — role plays, a decent facilitator, a laminated card with the model on it. I used it twice. Then Q1 came in soft and my one-to-ones went straight back to being status updates, because that's what fits in twenty-five minutes when someone's chasing you for numbers. The card's still in my drawer. I know the questions, I just don't have the meeting to ask them in.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

The coaching day was, by the standards of these things, good. An external facilitator who had clearly done the job. Role plays that were only mildly uncomfortable. A model with an acronym, a laminated card, a workbook. People engaged with it. The feedback forms came back strong and you filed them.

Then February became March. A customer escalated, two people resigned in the same fortnight, and the forecast came in soft enough that everything above your managers' heads got louder. The one-to-ones did not stop — they reverted. Twenty-five minutes, status, blockers, next steps, done. Which is what they were before the workshop, and which is the format the calendar and the reporting rhythm actually reward.

Nobody did anything wrong. Every manager on that course could still describe the model accurately in June. They simply were not using it, and if you asked why, most would say something about not having the time — which is true, and is not the whole explanation.

Why a workshop cannot change a calendar

Training addresses capability. What limits coaching behaviour is mostly not capability. Gartner's manager development research finds that 45% of managers report they lack the confidence to help employees develop the skills they need, and that managers spend on average just 9% of their time developing direct reports. Those two figures point at different things. The first is a skill and confidence problem, which training can touch. The second is structural, and it is much larger.

A manager's week is allocated by whatever generates consequences. Escalations, reporting deadlines, approvals, their own manager's questions. Developmental conversations generate no consequence this week, this month, or usually this quarter. No meeting fails if you skip the coaching, no dashboard turns red, nobody follows up. Under load, everything without a consequence gets deferred — and management is a role that is under load approximately always.

There is a second mechanism, quieter. Coaching is a skill people are bad at before they are good at it, and being visibly bad at something in front of a direct report is genuinely uncomfortable. A workshop supplies a model but not repetition. Without repetition the discomfort never drops, so the behaviour never becomes cheap enough to reach for under pressure.

What the training spend actually buys

What it reliably buys is vocabulary. Six months later, managers will use the language from the course. They will say they are trying to be more coaching-led. This is not nothing — it shifts what people believe good management looks like — but it is not behaviour change, and it will not appear in anything the business measures.

The cost is the day itself: facilitator fees, plus a room of managers off the floor, which is the larger number and the one that rarely gets counted. Then it comes due again at the next budget round, when someone asks what the leadership development line delivered and the honest answer is a stack of positive feedback forms from eight months ago.

The cost that appears on no invoice is the direct reports. A person whose manager cannot help them develop will get that help somewhere else, or not at all. Not at all is the common outcome, and it surfaces later as a promotion pipeline with nobody in it who is ready.

Why refreshers and toolkits do not stick either

The standard follow-up is more content: a refresher session, an LMS module, a manager toolkit on the intranet. Each assumes the gap is knowledge. The managers already have the knowledge. Adding more of it is the cheapest intervention available and the least likely to change a Tuesday.

Making coaching part of the annual review sounds rigorous and produces documentation rather than behaviour. Managers write competent paragraphs about their coaching approach once a year. Nothing about how they run a Thursday one-to-one changes.

Buying an external coach for every manager works, and is priced accordingly. Most organisations can afford it for a senior tier, which is usually not the tier where the development problem lives. The problem lives with front-line managers, who are numerous, busy, and least likely to get anything individual.

What actually changes behaviour

  • Attach it to a meeting that already exists. Do not create a coaching conversation. Change the shape of the one-to-one already in the calendar — two developmental questions before the status update, every time.
  • One behaviour at a time. A model with four stages and eight question types is a workshop artefact. "Ask one open question and then wait" is something a tired person can actually do in a real meeting.
  • Prompt at the point of use. A prepared prompt landing the morning of the one-to-one, referencing what that particular report is working on, outperforms any amount of prior training. It removes the preparation the manager was never going to do.
  • Give managers somewhere to take the hard ones. The conversations they avoid are specific: underperformance, a promotion that is not coming, someone visibly checked out. Access to a person who will help them plan that conversation is worth more than a general course.
  • Make it visible one level up. If a manager's own manager never asks about development, the manager learns exactly what it is worth. The intervention is a standing question in the skip-level, not another module.
  • Measure the behaviour, not the belief. Whether developmental conversations are happening is knowable. Whether managers feel more confident is a feedback form.

The workshop was not wasted. It was the smallest part of the work, delivered as though it were the whole of it.
