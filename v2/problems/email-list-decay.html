An email list decays whether or not it is ever touched

Marketing & VisibilitySourced & verified

My list is about 900 people and I email it maybe four times a year, usually when I want something. Last send, roughly forty bounced — mostly people who'd changed jobs since I collected the address. Opens were down and I honestly can't tell if that's the subject line or just that half of them have forgotten who I am. Probably the second one.

The list sits there looking like an asset. Nine hundred people, built over four or five years from workshops, downloads, referrals, an old lead magnet. On paper it is the one channel nobody can take away from you — no algorithm, no platform, just a file of addresses belonging to people who once raised a hand.

Then you send something. Forty bounce. The open rate is lower than last time, which was lower than the time before. Two people reply asking who you are. One unsubscribes with a note saying they do not remember signing up, which is annoying because they definitely did, in 2022, at an event you both attended.

The natural conclusion is that the send was bad. Usually the send is fine. What has happened is that the list aged, and it did that whether you were emailing it or not.

Decay is the default state, not a punishment for neglect

Three separate things erode a list simultaneously, and only one of them is under your control.

The first is address churn. People change jobs and their work address dies with the role. Companies fold, domains lapse, personal accounts get abandoned in favour of a new one. None of this involves any decision about you. It is background attrition on any collection of contact details, and it runs continuously.

The second is memory. Consent has a half-life. Someone who gave you their address at a workshop remembers you for a few weeks, vaguely for a few months, and not at all after a year of silence. When you finally send something, they are not receiving an email from a person they know — they are receiving an email from a stranger who somehow has their address. That is not a subject-line problem. It is a recognition problem, and it produces the spam complaints that damage everything you send afterwards.

The third is deliverability. Mailbox providers judge whether to place your mail in the inbox partly on how people have engaged with your previous mail. A list that mostly does not open trains that judgement against you, so the people who would read start seeing you in promotions or spam. The engaged minority get punished for the disengaged majority — which is exactly backwards from what most people assume infrequent sending does.

Why nobody maintains it

List maintenance is unglamorous, invisible, and has no deadline. It is also work with no colleague attached, which matters more than it sounds. Leapers, in its 2024 Mental Health in Freelancing annual report, found that 71.9% of freelancers and solo practitioners felt isolated or lonely sometimes or frequently that year, and 90% felt it at some point — roughly three times the national average. That is the working context in which every one of these small, boring, ownerless tasks either happens or does not. There is no one to hand it to, no one who notices it slipping, and no colleague whose own work depends on it being done.

So it sits. And because the consequence of skipping it is invisible for months, it keeps sitting, until the day you actually need the list and discover it has quietly aged out from under you.

What it costs, and why the usual fixes fail

The cost lands at the worst possible moment. Lists get used when there is a launch, a gap in the calendar, or a new programme — that is, when you need results quickly. That is precisely when you discover the reach is a fraction of the number in the dashboard, and that the fraction is the least engaged version of it.

The common responses tend to accelerate the problem rather than fix it. Emailing only when you want something is the most damaging, because it trains recipients that your name in the inbox means an ask; over time the only people left opening are the ones who were going to buy anyway. Buying or scraping addresses to top up the numbers replaces slow decay with immediate damage to sender reputation. Keeping everyone forever because a big number feels safer preserves the dead weight that is dragging down inbox placement for the people who still care. And a single re-engagement campaign — the "are you still interested?" email — helps once, then leaves the underlying rhythm unchanged, so the same decay resumes the next day.

What a maintained list looks like

None of the fixes are clever. They are just consistent, which is the hard part.

  • A predictable rhythm with a low ask. Monthly is enough. The point is that people keep recognising your name, which is what makes the occasional real ask land at all.
  • Something worth opening most of the time. A short observation from actual client work beats a polished newsletter that arrives twice a year and asks for a booking.
  • Active removal. A sunset rule — anyone who has not opened anything in twelve months gets one last email and then goes — improves delivery for everyone remaining. Deleting subscribers feels wrong and is almost always correct.
  • Segmentation by source and recency. Someone who enquired last month and someone who downloaded a PDF in 2021 should not receive identical mail.
  • Cleaning as routine, not as a project. Bounces removed after every send, not once a year when it becomes a problem.

The list is not really a list of addresses. It is a record of attention you were given at some point, and attention expires unless something maintains it.
