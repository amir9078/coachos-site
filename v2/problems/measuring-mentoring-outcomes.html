Without a goal set at the start, there is no way to prove anything changed

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

Nine months in with someone and I genuinely think it went well. Then I sit down to ask for a testimonial and there's nothing to point at — no "here's where you were in March, here's where you are now". Because nobody wrote down where she was in March. The last three all sort of tapered off, a reschedule and then another one and then nothing, and honestly I can't tell whether they got what they needed or just couldn't tell either.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

The session runs long because it's going well. She's describing a conversation with her director that she'd have handled completely differently a year ago, and you can hear the difference in how she tells it. Good session. You write two lines in your notes and move on to the next thing.

Nine months later she stops booking. Not dramatically — a reschedule, then another, then a message about things being busy at work. You're fairly sure it worked. You'd like to ask for a testimonial. And when you sit down to write the request you realise there's nothing to anchor it to, because you can't say where she started. What you have is adjectives. More confident. Clearer. Handles her director better than she used to.

Adjectives are what's left when nobody wrote down the starting position.

Why the goal never gets set

The first session has one obvious job, and it isn't administration. Someone you barely know has to say the real thing out loud — that they're out of their depth, or that they can't stand a colleague, or that they took the promotion and now regret it. Producing a form and asking them to define success in measurable terms cuts straight across that. So you skip it, sensibly, and plan to come back to it.

There's never a natural moment to come back to it. By session three you're inside the work. Raising it then feels like auditing the relationship rather than doing it.

The second reason is that the presenting problem usually isn't the real one. People arrive saying they want to get better at presenting, and by session four it turns out they don't trust their own judgement in the room. Setting a target on day one can feel like committing to the wrong thing. That's a genuine risk, and it's also an excuse, because a recorded starting point stays useful even when the goal later changes.

The underlying mechanism is timing. Everything arguing against a baseline is felt in the first hour. Everything arguing for one is felt nine months later, by which point you can't go back and collect it.

What the missing baseline costs

Start with what mentees themselves report. Gallup's analysis in "Mentors and Sponsors Make the Difference" found employees with a formal mentor are 75% more likely to strongly agree their organisation gives them a clear development plan, and 38% more likely to say they have someone helping them reach their goals. Read that as a description of what mentoring feels like when it's working: not mainly good conversation, but a sense of direction and of moving towards something named. If nothing is ever named, that's the part you're leaving out.

Then the practical costs. You can't ask for a testimonial, because you'd effectively be asking the person to invent a before-and-after on the spot. Most will write something warm and generic that persuades nobody, and you'll feel awkward for having asked.

You also can't tell when you're finished. A relationship with no target has no ending, only a fade. That fade looks the same from the mentee's side — nothing wrong, nothing completed — and once there's no marker to reach, rescheduling starts to feel costless.

And you can't improve. With no baseline across your mentees, there's no way of noticing which kinds of people you're genuinely good for and which you should be referring elsewhere.

Why goal-setting templates don't fix it

The usual response is a form. SMART goals at session one, filled in politely, filed, never mentioned again. The mentee writes what sounds respectable rather than what's true, because at session one they don't yet trust you enough to write the true version.

End-of-programme feedback has the opposite problem: you're asking someone to rate a change that neither of you measured. You get a score out of ten and a sentence about how helpful the sessions were.

Session notes feel like the answer and aren't. Notes record what was discussed. They don't record distance travelled, and reconstructing a story from twenty sets of notes after the fact is a job nobody actually does.

All three fail in the same place. They capture activity or sentiment. Neither is a starting point, and without a starting point every claim about impact is just an opinion, including yours.

What a working setup looks like

Capture the baseline in the mentee's own words, at intake, before the first session rather than during it. A short form asking four things: what's happening right now, what's hardest about it, what would be specifically different in six months, and what would count as evidence that it changed. The last one matters most, and it should include at least one thing another person could observe. "I'd be running the Monday review myself" is evidence. "I'd feel more confident" isn't.

Then revisit on a schedule rather than whenever it comes up. Session four, session eight, and the close. Five minutes each time. Read back their original words and ask what's moved — their assessment, not yours.

Let the goal change, and record the change and the reason for it. A mentee who arrived wanting to fix their presenting and left having renegotiated their whole role is a better story than a target hit cleanly, but only if both versions were written down.

Set an end date at the start. The closing session is where a usable testimonial actually comes from: you read out what they wrote in month one, they react to it, and you ask whether you can quote that reaction. You're not asking them to remember. You're asking them to confirm.
