The handle available on one platform is already gone on the next

Website & PresenceSourced & verified

I'm one thing on Instagram, the same thing with an underscore on TikTok, and something completely different on LinkedIn because that was set up before the business had a name. A client told me she'd searched for me on TikTok, found an account using my old logo, messaged it and got nothing back. That account isn't mine. I don't know whose it is. The business cards I printed in 2023 have the underscore version on them, so those are wrong now too.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

You sign up for a new platform because that's where people are this year. You type in your name. It's taken — by an account with no posts, a default avatar and a last login sometime in 2017. So you add an underscore, or your city, or the word "official," and you get on with it, because it's a fifteen-second problem and you have a full day.

Do that three or four times over five years and you end up with a business that has a slightly different name in every place someone might look for it. You know all of them. Nobody else does. When someone hears your name spoken — in a session, at a networking thing, in a group chat — they get one shot at typing it into a search box, and the version they guess is the plain one you don't own.

Lucidpress's "State of Brand Consistency" report found that brands with consistent naming and presentation across every platform see revenue increases of 23% to 33% compared to inconsistent branding. The mechanism behind that isn't mysterious. Consistency removes a decision from the other person, and every decision you leave to someone who is mildly interested is a decision they can resolve by doing nothing.

Why names fragment

The fragmentation is structural, not careless. Handles are claimed sequentially, one platform at a time, spread across the years, and each signup happens at a moment when you're focused on something else entirely. There is never a single day when you sit down and choose a name for all of them at once, because most of them didn't exist yet when you started.

Platform rules make it worse by disagreeing with each other. Character limits differ. Some allow dots and some don't. Some allow hyphens, some strip them. A name that fits perfectly in one place has to be shortened or altered in another, which means even a disciplined person ends up with variants.

Then there's the squatting problem, which is mostly not malicious. Ordinary users register names they never use. Someone with your surname got there in 2014. A dormant account holds the exact name you need and there is no realistic way to reach the person behind it. Meanwhile every new platform triggers a land grab in its first months, and if you weren't paying attention that quarter, the good version of your name went to somebody who was.

And some of the abandoned accounts are yours. A profile made on an old phone, tied to an email you stopped using, with no password saved anywhere. You can see it. You can't get into it.

What it costs

Most word of mouth is spoken, not linked. Someone recommends you out loud, and the person listening types what they heard. If what they heard doesn't resolve to you on the platform they use, you're relying on them to care enough to keep hunting. Very few people do. It isn't rudeness — they were only mildly curious in the first place, and a second search feels like effort.

The scattered version also costs you recognition. Someone sees you on one platform, then encounters a different name with a different photo elsewhere and doesn't connect the two, so exposure that should have accumulated into familiarity stays as separate, forgettable impressions. That's the effect the Lucidpress figures are pointing at: consistency compounds, and inconsistency resets.

The sharper risk is impersonation. An unclaimed name that matches your business is an opening for anyone who wants one. Photos get scraped, a plausible profile goes up, and it messages your audience — usually about a giveaway, a coaching offer, or a payment. The people who get scammed have no way of knowing which account is real, and the damage lands on your name regardless of who did it.

Why the usual fixes fail

The full rebrand — picking a new name that happens to be free everywhere — solves the naming problem by throwing away the recognition you already have. Everyone who knows the old name has to relearn, links pointing at old profiles go stale, and you spend months explaining a change nobody asked about.

Buying a handle from a squatter rarely works out. Prices are arbitrary, many sellers are running a straightforward scam, and platform rules often forbid the transfer, meaning the deal you paid for can be reversed with no recourse.

Reporting a dormant account to reclaim its name almost never succeeds either. Platforms generally release names only for trademark reasons, and even then slowly. Waiting on that is not a plan.

And "I'll sort it later" gets more expensive with time, not less. Every additional platform adds another variant, every printed item locks one version in, and every existing profile carries followers you'd lose by starting again.

What a working setup looks like

If you're naming anything now, check availability everywhere before committing — including platforms you have no intention of using yet — and choose something that survives the strictest character rules you'll encounter. Then claim it in all of those places on the same afternoon, even the ones you'll leave empty. A claimed dormant account is cheap insurance against someone else holding your name.

If the fragmentation has already happened, don't start over. Pick one fallback pattern for the places where the exact name is gone and use that same pattern everywhere it's needed, so there's one predictable variation rather than five random ones. Then make everything above the handle identical: the same display name, the same avatar, the same one-line description of what you do. Recognition can travel on the picture and the display name even when the handle can't.

Make your own website the place that settles it, with links to every real account, so anyone who is unsure has one page to check. Keep a written record of every profile — platform, exact handle, login email, who has access — and go through it twice a year, closing what's dead and reporting impersonators with your verified profile as the reference. It's an hour of unglamorous admin that removes a whole category of confusion.
