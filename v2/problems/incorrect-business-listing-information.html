Wrong address or phone number on one directory, and clients show up at the wrong place

Website & PresenceSourced & verified

A woman stood outside our old unit on a Saturday morning texting me 'am I in the right place?' We'd moved in the February. I'd updated Google the week we moved so I assumed that was that — I didn't know there were six other places carrying the old address, some of which I've never signed up for in my life. One of them still lists a landline we gave up. She rebooked, to be fair, but I don't think everyone does.

You moved. Or you changed your number, or your hours, or you started running Tuesday sessions somewhere different. You updated the obvious places, told your existing clients, and got on with the work.

Months later somebody is standing outside a building you left, phone in hand, deciding whether you're worth another five minutes. Most people in that position don't call. They assume they've made a mistake or that you've closed, and they go back to the search results to find whoever else is nearby. You never learn this happened. The only trace is a booking that quietly didn't occur.

BrightLocal's "Local Citations Trust Report" found that 68% of consumers say they'd stop using a local business after finding incorrect information in its online listings, and 30% say they'd go straight to a competitor. What makes that difficult isn't the percentage. It's that being wrong somewhere reads as carelessness about everything else, and it happens on a page you don't remember creating.

Why the wrong details are there in the first place

Most of your listings weren't made by you. Data aggregators build business records from public sources — company registrations, phone directories, utility and mapping data, old scraped websites — and syndicate them outward to directories, map apps, sat-navs, review sites and voice assistants. By the time you've noticed a listing exists, it has usually been copied to several other places.

That's why fixing one page doesn't hold. If you correct a directory entry but the aggregator feeding it still has the old record, the old record often comes back on the next refresh. It looks like your edit was ignored. Really it was overwritten from upstream.

There's a second mechanism that catches people out: listings can be edited by strangers. Some platforms accept suggested changes from the public, and a well-meaning passer-by or a bot can update your hours or mark you as permanently closed. Notification of that lands in an inbox you don't check.

Then there's duplication. A previous employee claimed a listing with their own login. An agency you hired for three months created new entries rather than claiming existing ones. You yourself set one up on a laptop you no longer own. Now there are several records for one business, splitting reviews and contradicting each other. On top of that, small format differences matter more than they should: Suite versus Ste, St versus Street, a mobile on one page and a landline on another. To you those are the same. To a matching system they're evidence of two different businesses, or of a record it can't trust.

What it actually costs

The first cost is direct and easy to picture: appointments that don't happen, people who arrive late and flustered, and calls to a number that rings somewhere else. Every one of those is a client who had already chosen you and then had that decision reversed by a logistical detail.

The second is trust, and it's disproportionate. Wrong details do a strange thing to a buyer's confidence. If your address is out of date, they wonder whether the prices are too, whether you're still trading, and whether the reviews they just read are about a business that still exists in that form. This is where the BrightLocal figures bite — the reaction isn't "I'll ring and check," it's "I'll try someone else."

The third is ranking, and it's the one that compounds. Search engines assemble a picture of a local business from many sources at once. When those sources disagree, confidence in the record drops, and a business that can't be pinned down gets shown less often than one that can. So the same problem that turns away the people who find you also reduces how many people find you at all.

Why the usual fixes fail

Fixing Google alone is the most common approach and it's incomplete. Google is where most of your traffic comes from, so it's the right place to start, but it isn't where all of it comes from — and it isn't the source that keeps re-feeding the bad data to everyone else.

Fixing it once is the second failure. Listing data isn't a document, it's a flow. Records get refreshed, suggested edits get accepted, and new listings appear for businesses that never asked for them. Accuracy on a Tuesday tells you nothing about accuracy in nine months.

Bulk submission services fail in a more damaging way. Handing your details to a service that pushes them out to fifty directories often creates a fresh listing alongside the stale one it should have claimed. You end up with two competing records per site, and the old one keeps its reviews and its inbound links while the new one has neither.

The last one is subtle: deleting duplicates instead of merging them. Deleting throws away reviews and history, and on some platforms the removed listing simply reappears from the underlying data. Merging preserves what the duplicate had accumulated.

What a working setup looks like

Write down one canonical record and treat it as the only version. Exact business name as you actually trade, address in one fixed format down to the abbreviation, one primary phone number that a person answers, hours including the seasonal exceptions, your categories, and one website URL. Everything else is checked against that document rather than against memory.

Fix upstream first so corrections stop being reverted, then claim rather than create wherever a listing already exists. Merge duplicates instead of deleting them. Make your own website match exactly — contact page, footer, and any structured data — because that's what cross-referencing systems compare everything else against.

Then keep it under review. A quarterly check across your main listings, alerts switched on for suggested edits, and a written plan for the next time something changes: a move or a new phone number is a project with a list, not a single edit made on a Friday afternoon. Most of this work is dull and repetitive, which is precisely why it stops getting done.
