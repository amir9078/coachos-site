Most companies never reply to a submitted lead at all

Leads & Follow-UpSourced & verified

A friend filled in our contact form on a Saturday as a test, pretending she wanted coaching for her team. Nothing came out the other end. No confirmation to her, no notification to me — the alerts had been going to an old shared address nobody had logged into since we changed who handled admin. Best guess is about three weeks. I still don't know how many, because there's no record of what never arrived.

Somebody fills in your contact form on a Saturday. They write two paragraphs about a team that isn't working, mention a rough budget, and press send. Nothing happens. Not on their side — they get the little "thanks, we'll be in touch" message — and not on yours, because the notification is going to an address that stopped being checked when you reorganised who handles admin.

They wait until Wednesday. Then they assume you're either busy or not interested, and they go with the third name on their list, who replied on Sunday morning. You never learn any of this happened. There is no bounce, no error, no gap in a report. The only trace is an absence, and absences don't show up in dashboards.

Most people assume this is rare, or the kind of thing that happens to careless businesses. It isn't. It's the single most common failure in inbound enquiry handling, and it is almost always invisible from the inside.

How a business ends up never replying at all

The scale of it is worth sitting with. A 2024 mystery-shop by RevenueHero, which submitted contact requests to 1,000 B2B companies, found that 63.5% never responded at all. Not late — never. Drift's State of Conversational Marketing research separately found 55% of companies take more than five business days to respond, and only 7% respond within the first five minutes. These are businesses actively paying to generate the enquiries they then don't answer.

Nobody decides to do this. It emerges from three ordinary mechanisms.

The first is silent breakage. Forms are a chain — plugin, mail relay, notification address, spam filter, CRM routing rule — and every link fails quietly. A theme update changes a field name. A deliverability change sends your own notifications to junk. An address gets deactivated when someone leaves. Nothing errors, because from the form's point of view nothing went wrong: it accepted the submission and said thank you.

The second is the ambiguous pile. An enquiry arrives that needs a real answer — a question about scope, a request you'd have to think about, someone whose budget is obviously too low. You mentally mark it as "deal with properly later." Later doesn't come as a scheduled event. Two weeks pass and now replying feels worse than not replying, so you don't, and it quietly converts from delayed to never.

The third is diffusion. In any setup with more than one person, an enquiry that arrives in a shared destination is presumed handled by somebody. Nobody is wrong, exactly. Nobody is responsible either.

What the silence actually costs

The lost enquiry is the smallest part of it.

The bigger cost is that non-response is read as character. Somebody who hears nothing doesn't conclude that your form is broken — that possibility rarely occurs to anyone. They conclude that you don't reply to people, and that is a judgement about how you'd treat them as a client. It's also the version they'll repeat if your name comes up in a conversation later, which is how one broken notification address quietly becomes a reputation.

The second cost is that it corrupts everything you think you know about your marketing. You measure form submissions, traffic and conversion rate. If replies aren't measured, a channel that produces excellent leads which never get answered looks identical to a channel that produces rubbish. Perfectly good campaigns get switched off on the strength of a failure that had nothing to do with them.

The third is compounding. The enquiries most likely to fall into the ambiguous pile are the interesting ones — the awkward, high-value, needs-a-conversation cases. The easy ones get answered because they're easy to answer. So the leads you silently drop skew towards the work you most want.

Why the usual fixes fail

Most people, once they've been burned, do one of three things.

  • They test the form once. They submit a test, it arrives, they relax. But breakage happens later, at a plugin update or a mailbox change, and a single test proves the form worked on the afternoon you tested it and nothing more.
  • They add an autoresponder. This is actively counterproductive as a safety net, because it makes the failure invisible from the outside as well as the inside. The enquirer receives a confirmation and reasonably concludes you got it, so they don't chase. Now nobody knows.
  • They assume somebody would have said something. People who get no reply almost never follow up to complain. They just go elsewhere. The absence of complaints is not evidence that responses are going out.

All three share one flaw. They treat replying as a behaviour that happens by default, and check for exceptions. In reality the reply is the exception, and it needs to be the thing that's tracked.

What a working setup looks like

Three changes cover almost all of it.

Make the reply an event, not a courtesy. Every submission should produce a logged record with a timestamp for when it arrived and a timestamp for when a response went out. A lead with a blank second timestamp is an open item, visible, and it stays visible until it isn't blank. If your setup can't currently produce a list of enquiries with no reply attached, that's the first thing to build.

Run a heartbeat. Submit a real test through your live form on a schedule — weekly is plenty — and confirm it reaches the destination and produces a reply. This is the only thing that catches silent breakage, because silent breakage announces itself in no other way.

Give the ambiguous ones a default answer. Most of the "deal with it later" pile can be closed with a short honest reply: this isn't something we do, here's who does; or, this needs a proper conversation, here are two times. A short reply that's slightly imperfect beats a perfect one that never gets written, and it removes the pile that would otherwise turn into silence.

CoachOS treats the reply as a tracked event rather than an intention: AI drafts a response to every submission and flags anything that hasn't been answered, and a human specialist reviews the drafts and the routing before they ship. Nothing sits in an unwatched inbox waiting to be noticed.
