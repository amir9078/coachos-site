Plenty of people want this. There is no system connecting them to it

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

I've got maybe six hours a month I'd happily give to this and nowhere obvious to put them. Everyone keeps saying demand is enormous and I believe it — I just can't see any of it from where I'm sitting. Someone messaged me in February asking if I "knew anyone", and I didn't, so that went nowhere either. It isn't a shortage. It's that neither side has any way of seeing the other one.

You have hours you'd genuinely give to this. Not unlimited — five or six a month, evenings mostly, and you'd enjoy them. You've said as much to a few people. Nothing has come of it.

At the same time, nearly every piece of advice anyone gives about careers or running a business includes the line "find a mentor", delivered as though mentors are standing about on street corners. The people repeating it rarely explain the mechanics, because there aren't any. There's no counter, no queue, no place where the person who wants this and the person offering it are both standing at the same time.

So you have capacity nobody can see, and somewhere there's a person who has been meaning to ask someone for six months and still hasn't worked out who, or how.

Why the two sides never meet

The numbers describe the shape of it. In an Olivet Nazarene University survey of 3,000 professionals reported by Forbes, 76% said having a mentor is important for career growth while only 37% actually had one. Research from SCORE and The UPS Store found a wider version of the same gap among small business owners: 89% of those without a mentor wished they had one, and just 25% did.

That gap isn't caused by scarcity. It's caused by the cost of asking. "Will you mentor me?" is a request for an unspecified amount of a near-stranger's time, over an unspecified period, with no obvious end. Most people can feel how large and shapeless that is, so they never send it. The ones who do send it usually aim it at someone conspicuously senior and busy, get no reply, and conclude the fear was correct.

The mentor side has a matching problem. Announcing availability feels like claiming an authority nobody granted you. "I'm available to mentor people" sounds, to your own ear, like a strange thing to say out loud. So you mention it privately to two friends and wait to be asked.

Both sides are waiting for an introduction. Neither publishes anything. That's the whole mechanism — not a shortage of goodwill, but the absence of any place where the offer and the need are written down in comparable terms.

What the gap costs

For you, capacity sits idle and then quietly disappears. Nobody keeps offering something indefinitely when nothing ever comes back. After a year or two of "happy to help if anyone needs it", you stop saying it, and the offer leaves the world without anyone noticing it was there.

For them, advice comes from whoever is closest rather than whoever is relevant — a colleague one step ahead, a founder on a podcast, a stranger in a forum thread. Proximity stands in for fit, and the substitution is invisible to the person making it.

For a practice you'd eventually like to turn into paid work, the gap means there's no pipeline, only luck. Enquiries arrive in a cluster and then stop for four months. You can't tell whether you're doing something wrong, because nothing is steady enough to read.

Why the usual attempts don't close it

Posting about availability once reaches the people who already know you, which is exactly the group that already knew you were available.

Open directories fail differently. A wall of profiles carrying a name, a photo and a job title gives nobody enough to judge fit. A mentee either picks on seniority or gives up entirely. Volume without structure raises the cost of choosing rather than lowering it.

"Book a free chat" links look like an answer and mostly produce no-shows. The ask is still undefined, so someone books on a Tuesday while feeling motivated and cancels on Thursday when the vagueness catches up with them.

Each of these leaves both costs exactly where they were: the cost of asking, and the cost of choosing.

What a working setup looks like

Publish availability as something bounded. Not "I'm open to mentoring" but a specific, small, describable thing: two 45-minute conversations a month, for people in their first year of managing a team, booked as a pair. A bounded offer is cheap to accept and cheap to refuse, which is exactly why people will respond to it when they'd never have sent the open-ended version.

Make fit judgeable before contact. Write about situations you've been through and the kinds of problems you've sat with, rather than titles and credentials. Someone deciding whether to approach you needs to recognise themselves in the description. A job history doesn't give them that.

Use a short application rather than an open calendar link. Three or four questions about their situation and what they want out of the conversation. It filters out people who weren't really ready, and it makes the ones who are do the work of articulating the ask — which raises the odds they turn up and that the first conversation is worth having.

Then give it a route onward. Say plainly what happens if someone wants more than the free two conversations: what the paid version is, how long it runs, what it costs. Without that, the same gap reappears one level up, with people who now know you and still have no way to continue.

And be findable. A page stating all of the above, plus a presence wherever your kind of person already gathers — an association, a course cohort, a forum, an alumni group. The demand doesn't need creating. It needs somewhere to land.
