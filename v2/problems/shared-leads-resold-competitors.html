The batch cost real money, so you worked it properly. You rang the first record within a few minutes of it landing, notes in front of you, and the man on the other end said you were the third person to call him about it that morning.

The rest of the list went about how you'd expect after that. Two numbers that rang out. Someone who enquired months ago and had long since sorted it. Someone genuinely looking who already had two quotes in hand, so the call turned into a price conversation inside ninety seconds. Maybe one you'd honestly describe as an opportunity.

The instinct afterwards is to blame the execution — call faster, tighten the opening. Both are worth doing, and neither touches the real issue, which is what you bought rather than how you handled it.

Why the same person ends up on four call lists

Follow the money on the seller's side. Generating a lead costs them something: ad spend, a landing page, a call centre, an incentive to get the form filled. Selling that record once, at a price a small business will actually pay, frequently doesn't cover the cost of producing it and leave a margin. Selling the same record three or four times does. The shared-lead model isn't an accident or a scam bolted on afterwards. It's the arithmetic that makes the business work at all.

The second mechanism is how intent gets labelled. A form submission is one data point. Someone comparing prices out of curiosity, someone who filled in a quiz, someone whose partner filled it in, and someone ready to sign this week all land in the same database carrying the same tag. Whoever grades that intent is paid for delivering volume, not for the outcome you get, so there's very little pressure on them to grade it harshly.

This isn't theoretical. In 2023 the Federal Trade Commission's case against HomeAdvisor ended with an order to pay up to $7.2 million after the FTC found the company had misled contractors about lead quality, including selling leads from people who were not ready to hire. One of the largest operators in the category, and the complaint describes exactly the structure above.

What shared leads actually cost

The invoice is the smallest part. The real cost is the position it puts you in. When you're the third near-identical call of the morning, the quickest way to stand out is price, so you quote lower than you meant to. Do that a few times and the discounted number becomes the number in your head.

Then there's the time. An hour spent dialling records with a poor chance of connecting is the least productive hour in the business, and it's an hour not spent on the kind of contact that compounds — a past client, a referral partner, someone who already knows who you are.

The quiet cost is that it feels like a pipeline. Leads arrive, the sheet has rows in it, and so the slower work of building channels you own never gets started. When the vendor raises prices or the quality slips, there's nothing sitting behind it.

Why the usual fixes fail

  • Paying for "exclusive" leads. Exclusive is defined by the seller. It can mean exclusive within a category, or within thirty days, or exclusive to one brand while the same company runs three others.
  • Calling faster. It genuinely helps, and everyone else received the same advice. You end up in a speed contest over a contact who was never going to buy from any of the four of you.
  • Claiming refunds. The windows are short and the evidence requirements are specific. Credits, where granted, keep you buying.
  • Changing vendor. A lot of supply is wholesale and resold under several brands. New login, same records.

Each of these adjusts your behaviour inside a system whose economics require the lead to be sold more than once.

What a working setup looks like

Purchased leads aren't useless. They're a supplement with a ceiling, and they need to be measured like a purchase rather than trusted like a pipeline.

  • Ask in writing before you buy. How many businesses receive this lead, how it was generated, how old it is when it reaches you, and what a refund actually requires. A vendor unwilling to answer in writing has answered.
  • Measure cost per closed client by source, over a long enough run to mean something. Cost per lead tells you nothing useful about a shared lead.
  • Cap the spend. A fixed monthly ceiling stops a mediocre channel quietly becoming the whole strategy.
  • Qualify before you pitch. Ask who else they've spoken to and what they've been quoted. It ends the dead calls faster and stops you bidding blind against people you can't see.
  • Follow up for weeks. The other three callers stopped after one attempt. Persistence is the only durable advantage available in a lead everyone else also owns.
  • Build an owned channel in parallel — past clients, referral partners, your own list — so bought leads top up a pipeline instead of being it.

None of that turns a shared lead into a good lead. It turns it into a priced input you can make a decision about, rather than a monthly bill you keep paying because stopping feels worse.
