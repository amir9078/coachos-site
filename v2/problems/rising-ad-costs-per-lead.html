The same ad budget buys roughly half the leads it used to

Leads & Follow-UpSourced & verified

We were sitting at about £11 a lead in March. I checked on a Sunday night because I couldn't sleep and it was £26. Same ad, same audience — I actually went into the version history to see whether I'd changed something and forgotten about it. Now the same money brings in maybe half the enquiries and honestly a lot of them are worse. I keep meaning to sit down properly and work out whether it still adds up.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.
$27.66 Facebook's average cost per lead climbed 21% year-over-year to $27.66 in 2025. Google Ads' average reached $70.11, rising in 13 of 23 tracked industries. WordStream / LOCALiQ, 2025 Facebook & Google Ads Benchmark Reports

You set the daily budget in spring and for a while it worked. Leads arrived at a price you could live with, enough of them booked calls, and the arithmetic held together well enough that you stopped opening the account every morning. Then it stopped holding. Not a crash — a drift. The cost per lead ticked up a couple of pounds, then a few more, and you put it down to a slow week.

Now the same spend brings in about half of what it used to. Nothing in the account changed. Same creative, same audience settings, same landing page, same offer. You have been through the checklist twice looking for the thing you broke, and there isn't one. The only variable that moved was the price of the auction you are bidding in.

That is an awkward thing to accept, because most advice assumes there is a lever you haven't pulled yet. Often there isn't. Paid attention has been getting more expensive for everyone at the same time, and the businesses that come through it are the ones that stop treating cost per lead as the number they manage.

Why the price rises when nothing in your account changed

The platforms do not price leads. They run an auction for a person's attention, and the clearing price is set by whoever wants that person most on that day. You are not bidding against other coaches or other local firms. You are bidding against everyone who wants to reach a forty-year-old in your city reading about burnout at midnight — retailers, insurers, apps, universities, and every business that watched its organic reach fall and moved that money into paid.

Two things pushed more money into the auction at once. Privacy changes broke a large part of the tracking signal the platforms used to match ads to people cheaply, so the systems need more budget and more time to find the same buyer. And producing ads got very cheap, so the number of advertisers and the volume of creative both went up while the supply of human attention stayed exactly where it was. More bidders, same inventory, one direction for the price.

The benchmark data reflects it. In the 2025 Facebook and Google Ads benchmark reports from WordStream / LOCALiQ, Facebook's average cost per lead rose 21% year over year to $27.66, and Google Ads' average reached $70.11, climbing in 13 of the 23 industries tracked. Averages hide a lot of variation, but the direction is the useful part — this is not something happening only inside your account.

What the increase actually costs

The obvious cost is margin. If a lead used to cost a fifth of your fee and now costs closer to half, either the price goes up or the work stops being worth doing. Most people find this out late, because they watch spend and lead volume rather than the cost of an actual booked client.

The less obvious cost is that everything downstream now has to work harder. At a low cost per lead, a slow reply is annoying. At three times that price, an enquiry you answer two days later is money set on fire. Sloppy follow-up, a form filled in on a Saturday that nobody sees until Monday, a no-show you never chased — all of that used to be absorbed by cheap volume. It isn't absorbed any more. Rising prices don't only make leads dearer, they make every gap in your process expensive.

Then there is the dependency. When ads are the pipeline, switching them off means having no pipeline, so you keep paying a rising price for a channel you can't leave.

Why the usual fixes stop working

The standard responses all help a bit, and none of them touches the mechanism.

  • New creative. Real, and temporary. A fresh angle pulls the cost down for a few weeks until fatigue sets in, and you are back to producing more ads for the same result.
  • Tighter targeting. A smaller audience is usually a more contested one. Narrow far enough and you pay a premium per impression for the privilege.
  • Switching platform. Cheaper channels are cheap because fewer advertisers have arrived yet. That window closes, and you have rebuilt everything to land in the same dynamics.
  • Cutting spend and doing organic instead. Reasonable, but slow, and organic reach is rationed too. It's a project measured in quarters, started at the moment you need clients this month.

None of these change the underlying position: you are renting attention at a price other bidders set, and you own nothing at the end of the month.

What a working setup looks like

The one part of this that nobody else prices is what happens after the lead arrives. That's where the room is.

  • Measure cost per booked call and cost per client, by source. Cost per lead isn't a business number. Two sources at an identical cost per lead can look completely different once you count who actually turns up.
  • Close the response gap. Every enquiry answered the same day, including evenings and weekends. Nothing else you can do returns as much on expensive traffic.
  • Follow up well past the first attempt. Most enquiries who eventually buy are not ready on the day they fill the form. A sequence that keeps contact over weeks recovers leads you already paid full price for.
  • Work the list you already own. Past clients, people who enquired last year, referral sources. Those leads are paid for, and most businesses contact them once and never again.
  • Give ads a narrower job. Retargeting warm traffic or filling one specific cohort, rather than carrying the whole top of the funnel.

None of this makes the auction cheaper. It changes how much of each expensive lead you convert, which is the only side of the trade you control.
