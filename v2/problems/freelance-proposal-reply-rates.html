Applying costs money, and most applications get no reply at all

Community & MarketplacesSourced & verified

Sixteen proposals in a fortnight. Two replies, one of which was "what's your rate" and then nothing after that. I keep a note of it in a spreadsheet because otherwise I'd talk myself into thinking it was going fine. The bit that gets me isn't really the money, it's writing something specific about somebody's project at half eleven at night and then watching the job close without a single hire.

You top up the credits. A job posts that genuinely fits — the right size, the right industry, a brief that suggests the person has thought about it. You read it twice, write something specific about their situation rather than about yourself, attach the most relevant example you have, and send.

Nothing. Not a rejection, not a question, nothing. Two weeks later the job closes with no hire recorded, and you never find out whether your proposal was read, whether the budget vanished, or whether they were only ever collecting quotes to show someone internally.

You do this a dozen more times, and the only feedback the system gives you is a slowly declining credit balance.

Why so many proposals go unanswered

The structure of a bidding marketplace puts the entire cost of the transaction on one side. Posting a job is free. Applying is not. That asymmetry is the whole explanation, and it is worth understanding without moralising about it, because the buyers are behaving perfectly rationally.

If posting costs nothing, people post for reasons that have nothing to do with hiring you. They post to price-check before asking their boss for budget. They post to see whether the thing is even possible. They post because procurement wants three quotes. They post, get thirty responses within the hour, feel overwhelmed, read six of them, and close the tab. None of that is bad faith. It is just what free supply of attention-demanding messages does to the person receiving them.

Meanwhile the proposal you paid for enters a pile where your placement is driven partly by timing and partly by platform signals you cannot see or control. You are not buying consideration. You are buying the right to be in the pile.

GigRadar's Upwork proposal benchmarks, drawn from December 2025 to February 2026 data, put platform-wide reply rates on paid proposals at roughly 7 to 15 percent — which means a large share of purchased credits are spent on jobs that were never going to be won by anyone, let alone by you.

What it actually costs

The credits are the small part. The expensive input is the proposal itself, because the proposals that have any chance are the ones that took real effort — reading the brief properly, working out what the client is actually worried about, writing something that could not have been sent to anyone else. Templates are cheap to send, which is exactly why they don't work: everyone sends them and buyers have learned to spot them instantly.

So the good proposal is the costly one, and the costly one is the one you fire into silence. Do that repeatedly and something changes in how you write. You start hedging effort. You start reusing paragraphs. You start sending more, faster, worse, which reduces your hit rate, which makes you send more still.

There is also a learning problem underneath all this. Silence carries no information. You cannot tell a rejected proposal from an unread one, so you cannot work out what to change, so people fall back on superstition — a new opening line, a different attachment, applying at a particular time of day. None of it is testable when the feedback channel is empty.

Why the usual fixes fail

Applying to more jobs is the obvious response and the worst one, because the cost scales linearly and nothing compounds. Fifty mediocre proposals cost fifty times as much as one and teach you nothing.

Bidding low wins a certain kind of job, and it is usually the job with the least budget, the least clarity and the most revisions — you have paid to acquire your worst client. Paying extra to boost placement adds cost to a bet whose underlying hit rate hasn't changed; it moves you up a pile that the buyer may not read at all. And polishing your profile helps at the margin, but the profile is what people look at after something in the proposal made them curious.

What a working setup looks like

The single highest-return change is qualifying before spending. Most freelancers decide whether to apply based on whether they could do the work. The better question is whether this buyer is going to hire anybody. Check their history — have they hired before, how recently were they active, is there a budget, does the brief read like a decision that has already been made or like someone thinking out loud? Skipping the ones that fail those checks costs you nothing and saves most of the waste.

Then send fewer, better, faster. The proposals that land are specific and early. That combination is only possible at low volume, which is the opposite of what the platform's economics encourage you to do.

Keep a plain record: what you sent, to whom, when, what happened. It feels like admin and it is the only way the reply rate becomes a number you can move rather than a mood you are in. It also tells you, within a couple of months, whether this channel is worth its cost at all.

And build one channel where the first message doesn't cost anything per attempt. Direct approaches to businesses you have a genuine reason to contact, former clients, people who already know your work — none of that has a per-proposal fee, and the research you do for a proposal can usually be reused there rather than thrown away when the job closes without a hire.
