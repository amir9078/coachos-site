Blog content increasingly gets written for nobody to click

Marketing & VisibilitySourced & verified

I spent most of two Sundays on a 2,000-word piece about how to price a programme — proper research, worked examples, the lot. It's had 31 visits since February and I'm fairly sure a chunk of those are me checking. I don't think the article is bad. I still think it's one of the better things I've written. Nobody's coming to it, is all.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

You wrote the piece properly. Not a listicle — an actual explanation of something you know because you have done it hundreds of times, with the caveats and the bits that people get wrong. It took two working days you did not really have.

Six months on, it has a few dozen visits. You check the search console and the impressions look fine, sometimes healthy. The clicks do not match them. The article is being seen and not opened.

The instinct is to assume you did something wrong: the title, the keyword, the structure, the length. Usually you did not. What changed is the page your article was competing on, and it changed underneath everyone at roughly the same time.

What actually changed on the results page

Pew Research Center's analysis published in July 2025 measured what happens when an AI-generated summary appears at the top of a search. Users clicked a traditional web result in 8% of those searches, compared with 15% of searches without a summary — roughly half the click-through. Only 1% clicked a link inside the summary itself. And 58% of the searches Pew examined from March 2025 produced one of these summaries.

Read that carefully, because it describes a structural change rather than a ranking one. On the majority of searches, the answer is now delivered on the results page. The reader's question gets resolved before your article is a candidate. Ranking first still matters, but first place on a page where almost nobody clicks is worth a fraction of what it used to be.

Worth noting what this does not mean. It does not mean your writing was not read — summaries are built from published material, so the work can inform the answer without ever earning the visit. It means the visit, and everything downstream of the visit, no longer follows from being useful. Usefulness and traffic have come apart.

What this actually costs

The obvious cost is the hours. A long explanatory piece is one of the most expensive things a solo practitioner produces, and it is paid for out of the same limited time as delivery. Spending two days on something that returns a handful of visits is not a small misallocation when you only get a few such days a quarter.

The less obvious cost is the false signal. Blogging used to have a feedback loop: publish, watch traffic, learn what resonates, write more of that. When traffic drops for reasons that have nothing to do with your writing, the loop starts lying to you. People conclude their audience does not care about the topic, or that they cannot write, and they drop the subjects that were actually their strongest — or they abandon written content entirely, which throws out the parts that still work along with the part that broke.

Then there is the compounding you lose. Articles used to accumulate: last year's post kept bringing people in this year. Where discovery goes through a summary instead, the archive stops working as a slow, steady source of enquiries, and the whole thing turns into a treadmill that only pays while you are running.

Why the usual responses make it worse

The first reflex is to publish more. If ten posts brought fewer visits than expected, try thirty. This scales the input against a mechanism that is not sensitive to volume — a summary appears regardless of how many articles are competing beneath it, and the click-through does not improve because you published more candidates for a click nobody makes.

The second is to write longer and more comprehensively, on the theory that thoroughness earns the visit. Comprehensive, well-structured, factually clean articles are precisely the material that summarises well. Making your piece easier to extract from does not make it easier to click.

The third is chasing keywords with lower competition. This gets you to the top of pages where the same thing happens, just with fewer readers on them. You end up optimising harder for a smaller version of the problem.

The pattern in all three is treating a change in how answers get delivered as a performance problem to be out-worked. It is not one, and effort spent on the wrong lever is the most expensive kind.

What still works

The useful reframe is to stop treating articles as an acquisition channel by default and start asking what each piece is actually for. Some purposes still work well and are not affected by any of this.

  • Writing for people who already know you. Something sent to your list, or linked in a follow-up email after a call, reaches a reader who chose to open it. A search page never enters the picture.
  • Writing that closes rather than attracts. The piece that answers the objection you hear in every discovery call is worth more sent to five prospects than published for five hundred strangers.
  • Material that cannot be summarised without loss. Your own case detail, specific numbers from your practice, an opinion that contradicts the consensus. A summary can flatten a how-to. It cannot substitute for a first-hand account.
  • Distribution before publication. Decide where a piece will be seen — email, a post, a community, a podcast conversation — before you write it. If the only distribution plan is search, treat that as a gamble rather than a channel.
  • Owned channels first. Anything that reaches people without an intermediary deciding whether to show it is worth more than it was two years ago, for exactly this reason.

None of this means stop writing. It means stop paying the cost of a search-led content programme while collecting the returns of something else.
