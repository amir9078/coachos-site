A missed domain renewal takes the whole business offline

Website & PresenceSourced & verified

The email went first. I thought it was Outlook being Outlook, restarted my laptop twice, then a client rang to say my site was showing a page of adverts. The renewal notices had been going to an address on that same domain, which — yeah. Card on file had expired in January and I never updated it. We got it back on the Thursday but the ten days of bounced enquiries are just gone.

It usually starts with email, not the website. A message you were expecting doesn't arrive. You assume the sender forgot. Then a client mentions that the one they sent you came back undelivered, and you check your phone and there's nothing wrong with it, and you restart things that don't need restarting.

The website comes next. Someone sends a screenshot: your address is showing a page of unrelated adverts, with a line at the bottom offering the domain for sale. It looks like you've been hacked. You haven't. A payment didn't go through, or a notice went to an address nobody reads, and the name you've been building on for years reverted to being available.

This is not a small-operator problem. Regions Bank — at the time the 22nd-largest bank in the United States — let its own domain lapse in 2013, and customers lost online banking access for close to a week. It was reported independently by DomainNameWire and TheDomains in April 2013. If an institution of that size can miss a renewal, the idea that this only happens to disorganised people needs to go.

Why renewals get missed

The most common cause is circular by design. Registrars send renewal warnings to the administrative contact on the domain, and for most small businesses that contact is an email address at that same domain. When the domain expires, the mail stops, which means the final warnings and the "your domain has expired" notice go into a mailbox that no longer exists. The system that's supposed to alert you breaks at exactly the moment you need it.

The second cause is the card. Auto-renew is switched on, everyone assumes it's handled, and then the card is replaced after a fraud alert or simply expires. The registrar attempts the charge, fails, sends an email about it that reads like every other registrar email, and gives up. Auto-renew was never the safeguard people think it is; it's an instruction that depends on a payment method staying valid for years.

The third is ownership. Plenty of businesses don't hold their own domain. It was registered by a developer during a project, by an agency you no longer use, by a former employee, or by a relative who was helping out. The renewal notice goes to them. Sometimes they forward it. Sometimes they've changed jobs, changed email, or lost interest, and nobody finds out until the site goes dark.

Layered over all of it is the fact that renewal reminders look exactly like registrar marketing, which you've spent years learning to ignore. The message that matters arrives in the same font as the message trying to sell you a privacy add-on.

What it actually costs

The website going down is the visible half and the smaller half. Email is worse. Every message anyone sends you bounces, including enquiries from people who will assume you've closed. Worse still, that address is probably the recovery contact for your bank portal, your booking system, your accounting software and your social logins. If you need to reset a password during the outage, the reset link goes to a mailbox that doesn't exist.

There's a reputational element too. An expired domain doesn't show a neutral error. It usually shows a parking page covered in ads, which reads to anyone who lands there as a business that has gone under.

Then there's the risk of not getting it back. An expired domain moves through a grace period, then a redemption period where recovery costs considerably more than a renewal, and then it drops and becomes available to anyone. Expiring domains are published lists, and there are services that exist purely to catch names with existing traffic and inbound links the moment they drop. If yours is caught, you're negotiating with a stranger to buy back your own name — and if you can't, every link pointing at you, every ranking you accumulated, and every business card in circulation now points at somebody else.

Why the usual fixes fail

A calendar reminder works right up until the person who set it changes role or leaves. It's a personal habit standing in for a business process.

Auto-renew on its own fails for the reason above: it's only as reliable as the stored card, and card failures are silent. Nobody rings you about a declined renewal.

"My developer handles it" is the most fragile arrangement of all, because it combines a single point of failure with an ownership question you won't discover the answer to until you're trying to fix an outage. If the domain sits in their account, your recourse depends entirely on them answering the phone.

Registering for ten years feels like a solution and mostly just moves the problem to a date when everyone involved has forgotten the domain exists at all.

What a working setup looks like

Hold the domain in an account the business controls, with two people able to log in. Set the billing and administrative contact to an address on a different domain — a plain Gmail or Outlook account is fine — so that renewal notices still reach you when your own mail is down. That single change breaks the circularity that causes most lapses.

Turn on auto-renew, then check the stored card once a year on the same day you do something else routine, and add a backup payment method where the registrar allows it. Enable two-factor authentication and the registrar's transfer lock, so the account can't be taken over by another route. Keep the domain registration separate from your hosting, so a dispute with one supplier can't cost you both.

Write down, in one place, which domains you own, which registrar holds each, who can log in, when each expires, and where DNS and email are hosted. Then put an automated check on top that looks at the expiry date and the certificate and tells an actual person well before either becomes urgent. The point isn't the reminder — it's that the reminder reaches someone whose job it is to act on it.
