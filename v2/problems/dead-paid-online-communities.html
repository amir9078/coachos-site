A paid community can still leave every real question unanswered

Community & MarketplacesSourced & verified

Seventy-nine quid a month and I've asked four questions since March. Two of them got a thumbs up emoji. One got answered by somebody who was clearly warming me up to sell me something. I ended up back on Google anyway, which means I'm paying seventy-nine quid for a Slack I've muted. I keep meaning to cancel and then there's a call in the calendar and I think, well, maybe that one.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.
90% Online communities consistently follow the 90-9-1 pattern, where roughly 90% of members never post at all. Jakob Nielsen / Nielsen Norman Group, "Participation Inequality," 2006

You joined for the room. Not the course material, which you could have found elsewhere, and not the calls, which you sometimes attend at 1.5x while doing something else. You joined because being able to ask a specific question and get an answer from someone who has actually done it is worth real money.

So you ask one. You spend a while writing it, because a good question takes effort — context, what you have already tried, what you are actually deciding between. You post it on a Tuesday morning.

By Thursday it has two emoji reactions and one reply from someone asking whether you have tried the thing you said in the second paragraph you had already tried. You solve it yourself, badly, and go back to searching. The subscription renews.

Why nobody answers

This is not a failure of your particular community. It is the default behaviour of every group of people online, and it has been documented for a long time. Jakob Nielsen's "Participation Inequality" work at Nielsen Norman Group, published in 2006, described the 90-9-1 pattern that online communities consistently follow: roughly 90% of members never post at all, a small fraction contribute occasionally, and a tiny minority produce most of the activity.

Once you accept that, the maths of a paid community stops being mysterious. Asking is cheap and answering is expensive — a real answer requires reading the situation, thinking, and writing, for no reward beyond goodwill. The people capable of giving good answers in a specialist community are a small subset of an already small group of posters, and they are usually the busiest members. They answer generously for a while and then they stop, because nothing in the structure replenishes them.

Growth makes it worse rather than better. Every new member adds potential questions immediately and potential answers rarely, so the ratio of asked to answered gets steadily worse as the community succeeds commercially. The founder who answered everything in month two cannot answer everything in month twenty, and the moment they stop, the visible norm becomes silence.

There is one more mechanism that quietly kills value: answers don't accumulate. A good reply is buried in a thread, in a channel, under a week of chat, and community search is almost always poor. So the same question is asked four times a year and answered well once, and no member can find that once.

What the dues actually buy

The subscription is the smaller cost. The real one is the delay. You had a decision to make on Tuesday and you were still stuck on Thursday, which means the question either got solved worse or didn't get solved. That is the thing you were paying to avoid.

There is also a slow shift in how you relate to the group. It moves from a resource to an obligation — a tab you check out of guilt, a notification you mute, a line on the card statement you notice and postpone dealing with. People rarely cancel in anger. They cancel while tidying up their expenses, having already stopped opening it weeks earlier.

For anyone running a community, this is the churn that hurts most, because it is your most engaged members who leave first. They are the ones who asked real questions and noticed the room could not answer them.

Why the usual fixes fail

Gamification rewards posting rather than answering well, and the two are not the same activity. Badges reliably produce more low-value posts from people who were already posting.

Prompts of the day, win-of-the-week threads and introduction rituals generate visible activity that pushes genuine questions off the screen faster. A busy-looking feed of small talk is arguably worse than a quiet one, because it hides the unanswered questions in noise.

Adding channels splits activity that was already thin, so every room looks emptier than the single room did. And hiring a community manager helps with tone and housekeeping but not with substance — the questions that go unanswered are the specialist ones, and a moderator who cannot answer them can only nudge others to, which is the problem you started with.

What a working setup looks like

The change is to stop treating answers as something that emerges from the group and start treating them as something someone is accountable for. Every question gets a real response from a named person within a stated window. Not a promise in the welcome document — an actual owner and an actual clock. Whether other members chip in becomes a bonus rather than the mechanism.

Then make answers compound. Anything answered well once gets rewritten into a short, findable reference that lives outside the chat, so the fourth person to hit the same wall finds it in ten seconds. Over a year this is what separates a community that gets more valuable from one that gets noisier.

Structure beats open access for the parts that actually change behaviour. Small cohorts, fixed pairings, sessions with a defined outcome — these produce participation from people who would never post in a general channel, because the expectation is specific and the audience is small enough not to be intimidating.

And measure the right thing. Posts per week tells you almost nothing. Percentage of questions that received a genuinely useful answer, and how long that took, tells you whether the room does what people are paying for.

CoachOS keeps a community answerable rather than merely busy: AI monitors for questions that have gone unanswered and drafts a first response, and a human specialist reviews it before it posts. Answered questions get turned into a searchable reference so the same question doesn't have to be answered from scratch every quarter.
