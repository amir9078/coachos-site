"Just 20 minutes" with no intake process eats an entire weekend

It started as twenty minutes. A call on Thursday that ran to fifty, because the first half went on working out what they were actually asking. Then a document arrived on Friday night. Then Sunday afternoon went on rewriting sections of it at the kitchen table.

None of the individual pieces were unreasonable. That's the point. Every single one was small, and each was easy to agree to on its own terms.

You've now got four of these running. Last month you turned down a paid piece of work because the calendar looked full, and when you looked properly at what was in it, most of it was this.

Why twenty minutes becomes a weekend

Without an intake step, you absorb the framing work. The person arrives with a fuzzy problem — "I'd love to pick your brain about my career" — and the first stretch of the call goes on establishing what they're actually stuck on. By the time it's clear, the useful conversation hasn't started yet, so you offer another call. That's how one conversation becomes a relationship without anyone deciding it should.

The second mechanism is scope. Scope gets set by the person with the least information about how long things take, and gets absorbed by the person who feels responsible. The mentee has no idea that "could you take a quick look" means two hours. You do, and you say yes anyway, because refusing feels wildly disproportionate to a request that small.

Then there's the open loop. An unfinished, undefined request doesn't stay inside the meeting. You carry it around, think about it in the shower, and end up doing work between sessions that nobody asked for and nobody sees.

This isn't a rare or precious complaint. A Nursing Times survey of 199 mentors found 70% citing lack of time as a barrier to mentoring and 67% citing direct conflict with their primary job. The constraint isn't reluctance. It's collision with the work that pays.

What it actually costs

Paid work is the first thing displaced, and it's displaced invisibly. You never decline a client because of mentoring. You decline because the week looks full. The unpaid, unbounded commitments got in first precisely because they were the easiest things to say yes to.

Quality goes next. Prep at eleven at night is worse prep. You turn up to the paid work slightly depleted and to the mentoring slightly resentful, and both parties notice something is off without either naming it.

Then there's the ending. Mentors who overcommit rarely scale back — they withdraw completely. They stop volunteering, stop answering the "can I pick your brain" messages, and the reason they give is that they're too busy, which is true and also not the actual problem.

The mentee loses too, which is easy to miss from your side. An unbounded relationship has no urgency and no finish line. Nobody prepares properly for a conversation that can always happen again next month.

Why willpower fixes fail

"I'll be firmer next time" is applied at the wrong point. By the time you're being asked for the extra thing, you've already agreed to an undefined arrangement. The leak happened upstream, at the original yes, and no amount of firmness downstream closes it.

Blocking time in the calendar protects you from meetings, not from the work between them. The document you promised to read doesn't need a slot. It takes the evening either way.

Refusing all of it isn't what you want either. You like this work, and you're good at it. The problem isn't that you're doing it. The problem is that it has no edges.

A booking link on its own makes things slightly worse, because it lowers the cost of making a vague request without doing anything about the vagueness.

What a working setup looks like

Put a short form in front of every first conversation. Four questions: what's the situation, what have you already tried, what would a good outcome from this conversation be, and is there a deadline. That form does two jobs. It moves the framing work to the person who actually holds the information, and it filters — someone unwilling to spend eight minutes writing it down was not ready to use your hour.

State the shape in the confirmation. Length, how many sessions, what's included. "Two 45-minute calls, three weeks apart" is a complete arrangement. "Let's have a chat" is an open account with no closing date.

Use a standing slot rather than ad-hoc scheduling. Every reschedule negotiation is its own small tax, and ad-hoc arrangements generate them endlessly.

Make no-prep the default on both sides. You bring your experience; they bring the material and the notes. If reviewing documents is something you're genuinely willing to do, it belongs in the paid version, named as such, rather than leaking into Sunday.

Close each engagement in writing: what was discussed, what they agreed to do, and what happens next or that nothing does. The written close is what shuts the open loop. It's the difference between a bounded piece of help and an indefinite standing obligation.

Finally, decide your capacity as a number — how many at once — and have an answer ready for the person who wants more than that. The answer can be a waiting list, a referral, or a price. It just can't be silence followed by a yes.
