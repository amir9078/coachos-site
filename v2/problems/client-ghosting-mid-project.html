Clients disappear mid-project with effectively no recourse

Community & MarketplacesSourced & verified

Three weeks of work, two rounds of revisions, then nothing. Sent the invoice on a Thursday, chased the Monday after, chased again the Monday after that. The last message in the thread is mine and it's been read. I still don't know whether something happened at their end or whether he just decided not to deal with it, and honestly the not knowing takes up more room in my head than the money does.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.
72% 72% of self-employed professionals experienced client ghosting and 71% faced late payments during 2024. Leapers, 2024 "Supporting the Mental Health of the Self-Employed" report (715 UK freelancers)

It is rarely dramatic. There is no argument, no complaint, no email saying they have changed direction. The replies just get slower, then shorter, then stop. The last message in the thread is yours and it has a read receipt on it.

Meanwhile you have delivered most of the work. You cleared a fortnight for this. You turned down something else because the dates clashed. You have an invoice out and a bank balance that assumes it gets paid, and the only tool you have is sending another message that will also be read and also not answered.

What makes it corrosive is not really the money. It is that there is no process to follow, nobody to escalate to, and no way to find out what happened.

Why clients actually disappear

Almost none of this is a plan to avoid paying you. The most common causes are internal events you cannot see. The budget got frozen. The person who championed the project moved teams or left. A reorganisation pushed the work down the priority list. Someone above them asked a question nobody can answer yet, and the project is now in limbo that has no name.

In every one of those cases the client owes you an awkward message. And awkward messages get postponed, and each day of postponement makes the message harder to write, until not writing it becomes the settled position. Silence is the path of least resistance for someone who has already mentally closed the project.

The structural problem is that you are outside their system. You are not in the standup, not on the internal channel, not copied on the email where the budget got pulled. An employee would have known within a day. You find out through absence, which takes weeks.

It is also close to universal. The Leapers 2024 report "Supporting the Mental Health of the Self-Employed", which surveyed 715 UK freelancers, found 72% had experienced client ghosting and 71% had faced late payments during the year. If it has happened to you, it is not a signal about your work or your judgement of clients.

What it costs

The unpaid work is the visible loss. The tail is longer than people expect. There is the time spent chasing, which is unbillable and unpleasant and tends to happen in the evening. There is the pipeline you cleared for a project that then evaporated, which means the gap shows up a month later. There is the accountant, the VAT position, the question of whether to write it off.

Then there is what it does to the next deal. People who have been ghosted tend to overcorrect — discounting to secure work, over-delivering to keep a client sweet, avoiding asking for a deposit because they are afraid of losing the job. All of which increases exposure rather than reducing it. One bad experience quietly reshapes the terms of every engagement that follows.

Why the usual fixes fail

A contract is necessary and it is not a remedy on its own. Its strength depends on your willingness to enforce it and on whether enforcement costs less than the invoice is worth, which for most freelance-sized invoices it does not. Clients who go quiet often know this without ever thinking about it explicitly.

Fifty percent upfront is good practice and it is only half a solution. On a short job it covers you. On a three-month project it means you are exposed on the back half, which is exactly where ghosting happens, because that is when the client's enthusiasm has faded and the deliverable is nearly in hand.

Chasing harder does not solve an unread problem. Adding a fourth follow-up to a thread that has stopped is more of the thing that already failed. And platform escrow only protects work done on that platform, which is no help for the direct clients most people would rather have.

What a working setup looks like

The principle is to keep your unpaid exposure small and continuous rather than large and back-loaded. Break the work into stages, invoice at the end of each one, and state plainly at the start that work on the next stage begins when the previous invoice clears. Not as a threat — as a scheduling fact, in the same tone you would use to describe a deadline. Clients who intend to pay find this completely unremarkable. It is the only mechanism that limits the damage without requiring anyone to enforce anything.

Build in a cadence that detects silence early. A short scheduled check-in, even a two-line status note, turns a three-week disappearance into a three-day one. Early silence is recoverable; late silence usually isn't. Write a pause clause into the agreement so that a stalled project has a defined state rather than dangling indefinitely — including what happens to the work and to the timeline if they come back.

Then run the follow-up sequence on a calendar rather than on how you feel. Fixed intervals, unemotional wording, a stated final step. The reason people's chase sequences fall apart is that each message costs something emotionally, so they get delayed, then sent all at once when frustration peaks, in a tone they later regret.

And keep enough live conversations going that no single client can take out a month. Diversification is not glamorous advice, but it is the difference between a ghosting being annoying and a ghosting being a crisis.
