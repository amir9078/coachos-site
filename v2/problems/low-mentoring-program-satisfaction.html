Only about a quarter of employees would actually rate the mentoring program well

Programs & StructureSourced & verified

We got 140 sign-ups, which for a company our size was a lot. The six-month survey came back and mentoring scored a 6.2 — same as the canteen. I read through the comments on a Thursday night and the most common one was some version of "my mentor is lovely, we just haven't met since March." Nobody was unhappy exactly. I think that's the bit I keep getting stuck on.

A composite account, written from the research. Not a quote from a named individual.

You launched with a hundred and forty sign-ups, which for the size of the business was a good number. The kickoff call had real energy in it. Matches went out inside a week, the goal-setting template actually got downloaded, and for about a month your inbox had thank-you notes in it.

Then the six-month pulse survey comes back and the mentoring question sits mid-table. Not bad enough to kill. Not good enough to put on a slide in front of the exec team. Most of the free-text boxes are empty, and the ones that aren't say some version of "my mentor is lovely, we just haven't met since March."

Nobody is angry. That's the harder version of this problem, because there's no single failure to point at and fix. The programme is running. It just isn't landing. If it's any comfort, this is the normal result rather than a local embarrassment — only 26% of employees would rate their company's mentoring program an 8 out of 10 or better, according to research from the HR.com Research Institute cited in MentorcliQ's mentoring statistics work. Roughly three in four people sit somewhere between "fine" and "I'd forgotten we had one."

Satisfaction is decided in the gaps, not in the match

Almost all programme design effort goes into the front end: the application form, the matching logic, the launch comms, the first-session guide. That's where the visible work is, and it's the part that can be finished and reported on. But whether someone rates the programme an 8 gets decided weeks later, in the silence between session two and session three.

The mechanism is unglamorous. A pair meets, it goes well, and they agree to grab time next month without booking anything. Six weeks pass. Now rebooking carries a small social cost, because whoever sends the message has to acknowledge the drift. The mentee waits for the mentor to suggest it, on the reasonable theory that the senior person's calendar is the constraint. The mentor has four other commitments with exactly this shape and doesn't suggest it. The pairing never formally ends. It goes quiet, and six months later both of them rate the programme a 6 out of low-grade guilt.

None of that is a character failure, and none of it is a matching failure. It's what happens when a relationship has no owner and no external prompt, competing for attention against work that has both.

What a mid-table score actually costs

The programme was almost certainly funded on an engagement or retention argument. A middling score doesn't support that argument and doesn't refute it either, so it sits there generating no evidence at all. Come budget season, "mentoring scored a 6" reads to a finance-minded exec as "we tried that, it doesn't work here." You then spend the next cycle defending a programme instead of improving it.

The second cost is quieter and worse. The people you need to re-recruit for cycle two are the same people who drifted in cycle one. A mentor whose pairing went nowhere doesn't write in to say so — they just don't reply to the sign-up email. Each cycle gets slightly harder to fill, the volunteer pool narrows toward whoever is most agreeable rather than whoever is most useful, and the quality spread widens.

And your name is attached to all of it. Programme ownership in HR and L&D is unusually personal. Leadership doesn't remember which vendor or which working group approved the design. They remember who stood up and pitched it.

Why the usual fixes don't move the number

The first instinct is better matching: longer application forms, more dimensions, an algorithm. This rarely helps, because the pairs that go quiet mostly aren't badly matched — they're unmaintained. A perfect match with no prompt at week six behaves the same as a mediocre one.

The second instinct is a bigger launch. A longer training session, a better handbook, a proper kickoff event. That front-loads support at the exact moment motivation is highest and support is least needed. By the time a pair genuinely needs help — when the obvious topics are exhausted, or when one of them wants out — the handbook is in a folder nobody can find.

The third is buying a platform. Platforms are genuinely useful for matching, scheduling and reporting, but most of them solve administration rather than attention. A dashboard telling you that a large share of pairs have logged no session since March is real value. It doesn't do the acting, though. Someone still has to write to those eighty people, individually enough that they reply.

The fourth is making it mandatory, which converts a satisfaction problem into a compliance problem and produces sessions people attend the way they attend fire training.

What a setup that holds looks like

Move ownership of the cadence off the pair. Sessions get booked because something in the system asks about them, not because two busy people remember. The prompt has to arrive before the gap opens rather than after — a nudge at week three is worth ten at week twelve.

Change what you count. Pairs matched is a vanity number. Sessions actually held, per pair, per month, is the number that predicts your survey score, and it's available continuously instead of twice a year.

Give pairs something to talk about at session three and beyond. Most relationships stall from lack of agenda rather than lack of goodwill, and a short prompt tied to where the pair actually is in the cycle does more than any amount of upfront training.

Build a no-fault exit. People stay in dead pairings because ending one feels like an accusation. If rematching is normal and framed as a scheduling reality rather than a judgement, you recover pairs instead of losing them silently.

Finally, collect signal as you go. Two questions after each session tell you which pairs are in trouble while you can still do something, rather than handing you a post-mortem in month six.
