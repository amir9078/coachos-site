The average business takes 42 hours to respond to its own leads

The quote requests land in an inbox that also collects invoices, newsletters, a supplier's shipping notifications and whatever else has your business address on it. You open it properly when there's a gap, which in a busy week means Thursday evening. Four enquiries are sitting there. The oldest one is from Monday morning.

You reply to all four that night, apologising for nothing in particular, because as far as you're concerned you answered the moment you saw them. One writes back to say thanks, they've sorted it. Not annoyed. Just sorted. They found somebody on Tuesday, and by Thursday you had become a slightly awkward email to answer.

Nobody designs this. It happens when the person who wants the lead and the person who has to open the inbox are the same person, and that person spends their working day actually delivering the work.

Why 42 hours is the norm rather than an outlier

Harvard Business Review published an audit of this in March 2011, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads", which submitted enquiries to 2,241 US companies and timed the replies across a dataset of 2.24 million leads. The average first-response time was 42 hours. Only 37% responded within an hour. Companies that made contact inside that first hour were nearly seven times more likely to have a qualifying conversation with the lead. And these were audited businesses with sales teams, CRMs and someone whose job title included the word sales.

If organisations with dedicated staff average 42 hours, it's worth understanding why, because the mechanism is the same one operating in a two-person practice.

Enquiries arrive as notifications rather than as work. A calendar booking has a time attached to it. An invoice has a due date. A lead has neither — it lands in a queue with no clock and no owner, alongside things that genuinely can wait. Nothing in the system distinguishes "someone is ready to spend money with you" from "your domain renews in 60 days". They look identical in a list of unread messages.

The second mechanism is that response work is interruptive by nature and the rest of your day is block-structured. You're in sessions, on site, in a workshop. The enquiry arrives at 10:20 and the first genuine gap is at 4pm, and by then there are six other things competing for the same gap. So the enquiry drifts into the evening, then into tomorrow, and the 42-hour average forms out of ordinary days rather than negligent ones.

What the delay actually costs

The obvious cost is the lost job. The less obvious cost is what the delay does to the conversations you do get.

When you respond first, you're the one who frames the problem. You get to ask what they're actually trying to fix, explain how you'd approach it, and set the terms of comparison. When you respond third, on day two, someone else has already done all of that. You're no longer having a conversation about their problem — you're being measured against a description of the work that a competitor wrote. Very often that collapses into price, because price is the only dimension left that's easy to compare.

There's a second cost that's harder to see. A slow reply is read as information about you, not about your week. Nobody thinks "they must be mid-session." They think you're either overwhelmed or not that bothered, and both readings make them less willing to hand over something important. You get treated as a fallback option before you've said a word.

Then there's the marketing cost. If you're spending anything at all on getting found — ads, SEO, referral time, a website rebuild — every hour that an enquiry sits unopened is spend already committed and slowly depreciating.

Why the usual fixes fail

The standard responses are all real attempts, and they all leak for the same reason.

  • Turning on phone notifications. Within a fortnight the enquiry alert looks exactly like every other alert, and you learn to swipe it away. Notification volume defeats notification urgency.
  • "I'll do enquiries first thing each morning." A batch process caps your worst case at roughly 24 hours, which is better than 42 and still far outside the window where speed does its work. It also breaks on the days when first thing is a 7am client.
  • Adding a CRM. A CRM records the delay beautifully. It doesn't remove it. Without an owner and a deadline attached to each lead, you've built a better archive of leads you answered late.
  • Handing it to whoever is free. Shared responsibility with no named owner means everyone assumes it's covered. Enquiries sit in a shared inbox precisely because they're technically everybody's.

What a working setup looks like

The goal isn't heroics. It's making a slow response structurally difficult.

Start by taking enquiries out of email entirely. They need their own queue where the only items are people waiting to hear from you, so that "is anything outstanding?" is a two-second question rather than a scroll through 40 unread messages.

Attach a clock to each one. A named owner and a stated target — an hour during working time is a sensible starting point — turns a vague intention into something you can actually miss and notice you've missed. Without a target there's no such thing as late, which is exactly how 42 hours happens.

Split the response into two moves. The first is fast and substantive: it acknowledges what they specifically asked about, answers the one question nearly everybody asks at that stage, and offers concrete times. That can and should be automated. The second is your actual reply, with judgement and pricing in it, which happens when you're free. What matters is that the person is no longer in silence while they wait for you.

Finally, review the timestamps monthly rather than the volume. Most people track how many enquiries came in. Almost nobody tracks how long each one waited — and that single number usually explains more about a quiet quarter than the traffic figures do.
